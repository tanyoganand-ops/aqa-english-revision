## Provisional study response

Kelman presents violence as something that surrounds Harrison, or Harri, and his community every day, and he shows its effects as fear, silence and a loss of innocence. Harri is an eleven-year-old from Ghana who arrives on a London estate, and his view of the violence is both innocent and honest, so the reader sees what he does not. I will argue that Kelman presents violence as damaging because it changes how people behave, so that fear and silence spread through the whole community and in the end take Harri himself.

The novel opens at a crime scene. A boy from Harri's school has been murdered outside a takeaway, and Harri stands behind a police line, fascinated by the blood. His reaction is that of a child. He also recalls that he "just wanted to get away before the dying caught us" (Harri, March). The verb "caught" makes death sound like something that chases, and it suggests that Harri senses a danger he cannot name. Because he treats death almost as a game he can outrun, the reader feels the irony, and the effect of the violence on Harri begins as a mixture of curiosity and dread.

Violence then enters Harri's daily life. The Dell Farm Crew try to bring him into their gang. X-Fire acts out a stabbing to show Harri what it was like for the dead boy, and later mimics shooting him in the head. Harri is blocked from the school cafeteria and made to take initiation tests, and in one of them he is talked into joining a mugging in which the victim is an elderly man from his church, Mr Frimpong. Harri feels sick, covers his face and runs past as the others crash into the old man. Afterwards he throws away the coat he was wearing and his Mustang toy as a sacrifice for what he has done. This is an effect on Harri's conscience. The violence does not only hurt its victims, because it pushes a child to take part and then to feel guilty, and the reader sees that innocence is under attack even when he is not harmed physically.

The effect is also fear. Weapons are never far away. Jordan, Harri's friend, shows him a knife, tells him to get one, and holds it to his face, and Killa takes out a screwdriver when a dog jumps up at him. Harri is chased, and Killa shouts a death threat at him. The word "DEAD" is later scratched into the door of Harri's home with a knife. In June, Harri dreams of the Dead Sea and wakes remembering that "Sharks never sleep. They have to keep swimming or they'll die so they're not allowed to sleep at all, not even for one second" (Harri, June). I read the shark as an image of constant alertness: even in a dream Harri cannot rest, and the boys around him live as if they were always being hunted. This suggests that violence has an effect on the mind as well as the body.

Violence also spreads into other relationships. Harri's sister Lydia is seen washing bloodstained clothes with bleach, and later Miquita holds a hot straightening iron to Lydia's eye and burns her cheek until she says she knows nothing. Lydia cries, destroys her dance costume and makes Harri promise not to tell anyone what he saw. Her fear becomes secrecy, and Harri, who wants to understand, is told to forget it. The effect on the family is that its members stop speaking honestly to each other. Harri's own behaviour changes too: he and Dean help to bully younger boys off a mattress at the park, and he throws stones at a bus with Jordan, which suggests that he is learning the community's habits. The pigeon says to Harri, "Violence always came too easy to you, that's the problem" (the Pigeon, April). The adverb "always" suggests that violence is a habit, and the pigeon's tone is a judgement as well as a warning.

The community's response is silence and resignation. Kelman links the book to the real killing of Damilola Taylor, and the BBC interview describes the police appeal in the novel as drawing only silence. In the novel, the one man who speaks to Harri and Dean says he does not know who did it, and a policeman visits the school to ask for information. Connor says that he knows who killed the dead boy, but the other boys do not believe him. When the playground is set on fire, the parents and children watch and complain about the local kids until the firefighters put the flames out. Harri reports that "They knew the fire would always win" (Harri, July). I read the fire as a symbol of the violence and the playground as a place for children, so the sentence shows people who feel unable to stop what they are watching. The modal "would always" suggests a belief that nothing will change. The effect on the community is a loss of hope, not only a loss of safety.

The cages show the same limit. Early in the book, Harri notices that "The baby trees are in a cage" (Harri, March). It is an ordinary detail from the estate, but it can be read as an image of young lives that are kept in and protected by force. It may suggest that children here grow up under restriction before they have a chance to choose.

The ending shows the cost most clearly. On the last day of school, Harri is happy: Poppy kisses him, and he runs home in an excited mood. Someone comes out of nowhere, stabs him on the steps and runs away. He calls for his mother, and the pigeon arrives to say that it will be over soon. Harri asks the pigeon to tell his sister Agnes a funny story from the plane, and struggles to keep his eyes open while he thinks of her. The violence that Harri tried to understand and to run from finally reaches him. It is in these last moments that Kelman shows the effect on a child most plainly. Harri's concern is still for his baby sister and not for himself, so the reader sees what is lost.

Context supports this reading. The BBC interview records Kelman saying that he was personally affected by the death of ten-year-old Damilola Taylor, who was stabbed on a Peckham estate in 2000, and that he drew on his own upbringing on a council estate. The study site's context page notes that Damilola was found in a stairwell, as Harri is at the end of the novel, and that police faced a "wall of silence" in that case. This is information from secondary sources, and I use it as background and not as proof of Kelman's intentions. It suggests that the novel's picture of fear and silence is based on a real case.

In conclusion, Kelman presents the effects of violence as widespread and lasting. It frightens Harri, pulls him towards guilt and secrecy, silences the adults and children around him, and finally takes his life. The effects on the community are shown less by speeches than by what people do not say and do not stop.
