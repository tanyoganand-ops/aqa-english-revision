# Question 3 (30 marks + AO4 4 marks): The Tempest

**Question:** see the official paper for the full wording (linked above).

### Model answer

Shakespeare presents Caliban as a victim, but not only a victim. The extract shows a man who is tormented and who answers with hatred. Across the play, Shakespeare lets us see the injustice done to him, and he also lets us see the harm Caliban would do.

In the extract, Caliban is tormented even while he is alone. He curses Prospero with "All the infections that the sun sucks up", wishing him "By inch-meal a disease", so his fantasy of revenge is slow and physical. The curse tells us how he feels about his own treatment. He explains that Prospero's "spirits" will "nor pinch, / Fright me with urchin-shows, pitch me i'th'mire", but "for every trifle are they set upon me". The list of torments is long, and "every trifle" makes the punishment seem out of proportion. The animals he describes, "apes, that mow and chatter at me / And after bite me", hedgehogs and "adders", turn the island he knows into a place that hurts him in every place he walks. He ends with "Do hiss me into madness", which suggests the pain is mental as well as physical. He is also afraid and obedient: "And yet I needs must curse" shows he cannot stop himself even though he knows what will follow. When Trinculo enters, he thinks the stranger is one of the spirits and decides to "fall flat" in the hope that "he will not mind me". A victim who hides from further pain is what we see.

Elsewhere Shakespeare gives Caliban a case. Earlier he tells Prospero "This island's mine by Sycorax, my mother, / Which thou tak'st from me", and he remembers that Prospero first "made much of me". His claim is that kindness was used to win his trust and then withdrawn. He sums up the cost of the arrangement: "You taught me language, and my profit on 't / Is I know how to curse." The line is sharp because it turns Prospero's gift into evidence against him. Caliban is shown as forced to serve, and he notes in an aside that Prospero's "art is of such power" that he "must obey". The word "must" is the language of someone with no choice.

However, Shakespeare does not let us sympathise without reserve. Prospero's reply to Caliban's claim is "Thou most lying slave", and he says he used Caliban "with humane care" until he "didst seek to violate / The honor of my child". Caliban does not deny it. He answers "O ho, O ho! Would 't had been done!" and says he "had peopled else / This isle with Calibans". The laugh and the wish are shocking, and they make his complaint about ill-treatment harder to hold on to. The play therefore asks how far he is a victim of Prospero's cruelty and how far of his own violence. Even Miranda calls him an "Abhorred slave", though that can be read as showing how little the family sees of him as a person.

Caliban's later choices show him as someone who looks for a way out and chooses badly. He greets Stephano with "Hast thou not dropped from heaven?" and takes a drunk for a god. By the end he can see his mistake: "I'll be wise hereafter / And seek for grace. What a thrice-double ass / Was I to take this drunkard for a god". The self-mockery is painful because it comes after he has been manipulated again. Prospero's final words about him, "This thing of darkness I / Acknowledge mine", are ambiguous. Prospero may accept responsibility, or he may only claim a possession. In either reading Caliban is still described as a "thing".

Shakespeare also gives Caliban the most beautiful language about the island. "Be not afeard. The isle is full of noises, / Sounds and sweet airs that give delight and hurt not" shows an imagination that Prospero's insults never allow for. That contrast makes the harsh treatment seem more unjust, because the play has shown that Caliban is more than a "demi-devil".

The play was written about 1610 to 1611, and sources link it to the English voyages to Virginia and Bermuda. The Royal Shakespeare Company notes that a letter by William Strachey about a shipwreck on the way to Virginia reached England in 1610 and influenced the storm, and Shakespeare's Globe links the play to the first English colonial settlements. In that setting, Caliban's claim that the island was his before Prospero came is read by many people as a comment on colonial seizure, and Prospero's language about him, such as "slave", is read as the language of a master justifying his rule. I am treating this as one reading that the text supports, not the only reading. Montaigne's essay on cannibals, which the RSC lists among Shakespeare's influences, argues that people who call others barbaric are often blind to their own cruelty. That helps explain why the play lets the audience see both Prospero's harshness and Caliban's violence.

In conclusion, Shakespeare presents Caliban as a victim of Prospero's control and of his own weakness, and the play never allows an easy answer.

### Notes (my reading, not an examiner result)

- AO1: one argument (victim of Prospero's control, with the case against him kept in view) across the extract and the play. Prospero, Miranda, Stephano and the island are all covered. The attempted assault on Miranda is dealt with as the reason the judgement is not one-sided.
- AO2: the curse and its list of torments, "every trifle", the animal images, "must", the language lines "profit" and "curse", "thing of darkness" and the isle speech are analysed as methods.
- AO3: the context is used to explain a reading of the text: the 1610 Strachey letter and the early colonies (RSC; Shakespeare's Globe), and Montaigne (RSC). No single context or topic is required. This answer does not discuss Ariel's treatment in detail, which is a choice of focus. The colonial reading is stated as a reading, not as Shakespeare's proven intention.
- AO4 (4 marks): spelling, punctuation and sentence variety. About 850 words, longer than a typical timed answer. Treat as a sample, not a target.
