# Question 2 (30 marks + AO4 4 marks): Romeo and Juliet

**Question:** see the official paper for the full wording (linked above).

### Model answer

Shakespeare presents parents and children as people who love each other and still cannot understand each other. Parents expect obedience and see their children as part of a family plan. The children turn elsewhere for help, and the result is tragic.

In this extract, Capulet's anger shows how fast his care for Juliet turns into control. He begins by describing his own effort: "Day, night, work, play, / Alone, in company, still my care hath been / To have her matched". The list of times and places makes his care sound endless, but the verb "matched" treats a daughter as a pairing to be arranged. Then he lists Paris's qualities, "Of fair demesnes, youthful, and nobly ligned", and the long, flattering description makes it sound like an offer Juliet should simply accept. When she refuses, his language collapses into insult. She is "a wretched puling fool, / A whining mammet", which belittles her as a crying child or doll. His threat, "hang, beg, starve, die in the streets", uses a list of four short verbs with strong stress, as if each one is a blow. Capulet also says "I'll ne'er acknowledge thee", so his love is conditional on obedience. Juliet's reply is simple in comparison: "Is there no pity sitting in the clouds / That sees into the bottom of my grief?" Her question is gentle and spoken to heaven because no human will listen. "O sweet my mother, cast me not away" shows a child pleading for belonging.

The play reflects a patriarchal society in which fathers were expected to have strong authority in the household, and marriages often served family interests. That helps explain why Capulet treats Juliet's refusal as a defiance of his role, but the play does not simply endorse it. Shakespeare invites us to judge him by his own words. Earlier in the play, Capulet speaks to Paris about Juliet differently: "Earth hath swallowed all my hopes but she; / She's the hopeful lady of my earth", and "My will to her consent is but a part". In Act 1 he says her consent matters, so his demand in Act 3 breaks the limit he set himself. The softer, protective father of Act 1 sits uncomfortably beside the one in Act 3, so Shakespeare suggests that Capulet's anger comes from a man whose plan has been threatened rather than from lack of love.

Lady Capulet is even colder. When Juliet kneels, she does not comfort her but says, "Talk not to me, for I'll not speak a word. / Do as thou wilt, for I have done with thee." The blunt, balanced sentences end with "done with thee", which sounds like rejecting a person, not guiding a daughter. Juliet is abandoned by both parents in one scene. That is why she turns to the Nurse, whose advice, "I think it best you married with the County", and comparison "Romeo's a dishclout to him", then fails her too. The Nurse, who has been a kind of substitute mother, now gives in to the family's world and its rules. Juliet must find strength and secrecy of her own, and that isolation helps push the plot towards the tomb.

The Montagues are shown more gently, but the lack of understanding is still there. Montague describes his son's behaviour with worry: "Many a morning hath he there been seen, / With tears augmenting the fresh morning's dew". The image of tears adding to the dew suggests he sees and cares about Romeo's grief, but he cannot reach it, and Benvolio, not Romeo's father, is the one who tries to learn the cause. So across both families, parents watch their children but do not talk with them honestly.

The ending shows what that control costs. In the last scene, Capulet offers his hand to his enemy: "O brother Montague, give me thy hand". The word "brother" is the opposite of the language of the feud, and it comes only after both children are dead. Montague answers by promising to "ray her statue in pure gold", and Capulet replies that Romeo's will be "As rich" and will lie "by his lady's", both "Poor sacrifices of our enmity". The word "sacrifices" accepts that the children paid for the adults' quarrel, and "enmity" names the cause. This does not undo Act 3: Capulet's behaviour there was his own choice, and the later change does not make it less harsh. What the ending shows is that both fathers understand the cost only when it is too late. The Prince says "A glooming peace this morning with it brings": peace returns only through the deaths of the next generation. In this way the play suggests that parents' control of children, and the feud that drives it, are repaired only when nothing can be repaired.

In conclusion, Shakespeare presents relationships between parents and children as bound by love, status and fear. Capulet's rage in Act 3 Scene 5 is the most violent example, but Lady Capulet's coldness, the Nurse's betrayal and Montague's distance show the same pattern.

**Commentary (my reading, not an examiner result)**

- AO1 (12): a single argument runs through the answer: parental love turns into control and the children are left alone. The interpretation is supported by quotation and kept to the task, with a clear judgement about Capulet. Both parts of the task are covered: Capulet and Juliet in the extract, then parents and children across the play. References are short and fitted into the argument.
- AO2 (12): methods are named only where they support a point: the list of times and places, the verbs in the threat, the contrast between the Act 1 and Act 3 Capulet, the balanced sentences of Lady Capulet, the "tears augmenting" image, and "sacrifices". Terminology is limited rather than piled up.
- AO3 (6): patriarchal expectations, arranged marriage and obedience are linked to specific moments, and the answer notes that the play itself tests those expectations (Capulet's Act 1 comment about consent). The feud is linked to the ending. No claim is made about how every audience reacts.
- AO4 (4): spelling, punctuation and sentence variety are controlled; no AO4 mark is guaranteed.
- Limits: the answer is about 800 words. The time the exam allows may not fit this length, so treat it as a full model, not a timed target. The Friar is not discussed, which is acceptable because the mark scheme says its listed points are not a required list.

# Question 7 (30 marks): The Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde

**Question:** see the official paper for the full wording (linked above).

### Model answer

Stevenson presents hidden lives and secrets as a danger that damages friendship and in the end the self. In this extract, Utterson meets secrecy in other people, and the novel suggests that the Victorian habit of hiding things leads to a darker secret life.

The extract begins with a letter that explains nothing. Jekyll's reply is "often very pathetically worded, and sometimes darkly mysterious in drift". The adverbs show that Utterson, and the reader, receive feeling without information. Jekyll then states that he will "lead a life of extreme seclusion" and asks Utterson to "respect my silence", so silence is not just a mood but a request, almost a rule of the friendship. The phrase "my own dark way" makes the secret a path he has chosen, and the repeated sense of darkness ties it to guilt. Jekyll says he has "brought on myself a punishment and a danger that I cannot name". The word "cannot" is ambiguous: he may be unable to tell or unwilling, and Stevenson leaves the gap open so the reader feels the pressure of the unspoken.

Utterson's own response shows that he is also a keeper of secrets. He is faithful to "professional honour and faith to his dead friend", which Stevenson calls "stringent obligations", so he does not open the envelope marked "not to be opened till the death or disappearance of Dr. Henry Jekyll". Here the packet "slept in the inmost corner of his private safe". The verb "slept" makes the secret seem alive but dormant, and "inmost corner" and "private safe" pile up places of hiding. As a lawyer, Utterson is bound to confidentiality; the profession of the novel's main investigator is partly a profession of keeping secrets. The sentences about the envelope also hold information back: the long sentence runs through the inscriptions on the packet before reaching "it was disappearance", so the reader waits for the point that unsettles Utterson.

This matters in the context of Victorian respectability, in which the reputation of gentlemen like Utterson, Jekyll and Lanyon mattered greatly, and in which public appearance could hide private behaviour. Early in the novel, the door to the building Hyde uses is "blistered and distained", with neither bell nor knocker, and it carries "the marks of prolonged and sordid negligence". The ugly door is a hidden face of the city, just as Hyde is a hidden face of Jekyll. Enfield's description of Hyde, "There is something wrong with his appearance; something displeasing, something downright detestable", shows how people sense something wrong without being able to name it, which echoes Jekyll's "cannot name".

The most direct statement of the novel's idea comes in Jekyll's final confession: "man is not truly one, but truly two". Here the hidden life is inside a single person. Jekyll adds, "Though so profound a double-dealer, I was in no sense a hypocrite; both sides of me were in dead earnest". He says that neither self was a pretence, which makes the secret more disturbing: the private self is not a mask over the public self but a real part of it. By structuring the novel as a series of reports and documents, with the full truth held back until the last two chapters, Stevenson makes the reader act like Utterson, piecing together a hidden life from fragments.

Overall, Stevenson shows that secrets begin as ways of protecting reputation and friendship, but they isolate Jekyll, hurt Lanyon, and finally destroy him. The extract is a small version of this: a friend is shut out, a letter hides more than it says, and a sealed packet waits to be opened.

**Commentary (my reading, not an examiner result)**

- AO1 (12): the answer keeps to one line of argument (secrecy as protection, then isolation, then self-division) and covers both the extract and the whole novel. Quotations are short. The interpretation (secrecy starts as protection of reputation and ends as self-division) is the answer's own supported reading, built from the extract and the novel, which is what the AO1 descriptor on an informed response asks for.
- AO2 (12): methods are analysed where useful: the adverbs and "cannot" in the letter, "slept" for the packet, the sentence that delays "it was disappearance", the door description, and the structure of documents. These points are my own analysis.
- AO3 (6): Victorian respectability and the professional role of a lawyer are linked to specific details. Other contexts (such as Darwinism or the Gothic) are not used; the mark scheme does not require any one context.
- Limits: the novel quotations other than the extract come only from the chapters that could be checked (Story of the Door, Search for Mr Hyde, Dr Lanyon's Narrative, Henry Jekyll's Full Statement). The answer is about 650 words and is not a timed target. The answer uses darkness imagery ("dark way", "darkly mysterious") but does not discuss light, and it leaves out Carew and the significance of Hyde's name. That is a choice of focus, and the mark scheme does not make any single point mandatory.
