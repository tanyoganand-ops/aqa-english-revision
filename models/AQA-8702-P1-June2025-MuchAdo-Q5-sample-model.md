# Question 5 (30 marks + AO4 4 marks): Much Ado About Nothing

**Question:** see the official paper for the full wording (linked above).

## Model answer

Shakespeare presents Hero as almost powerless in this scene, and he makes the audience see that the men take her power away. The wider play shows women with more voice than Hero has here, but a voice that the men do not always have to listen to.

In the extract, Claudio talks about Hero as if she were property being handed over. Leonato says he gave her "freely", and Claudio answers with the language of payment: "What have I to give you back, whose worth / May counterpoise this rich and precious gift?" A "gift" with a "worth" can be weighed, and so can be returned. When he says "There, Leonato, take her back again, / Give not this rotten orange to your friend", Hero becomes damaged goods. The insult "rotten orange" is about what is inside something that looks fine, and that is the accusation he makes about her.

Claudio also takes over the meaning of Hero's body. He says "Behold how like a maid she blushes here!" and then reverses what a blush means: "Her blush is guiltiness, not modesty." A blush can be read either way, so the argument cannot be answered, and she has no way to prove him wrong. He speaks to the onlookers, "All you that see her", and not to her. In the extract she says nothing. When she does speak later in the scene, it is a polite question, "Is my lord well that he doth speak so wide?", and nobody answers it.

What follows shows the same lack of power. Hero faints, and Beatrice says "Dead, I think." Leonato, her father, then takes the men's side before he has asked Hero for her account. He says "Death is the fairest cover for her shame" and "Do not live, Hero, do not ope thine eyes." His speech is full of the word "mine": "mine, and mine I loved, and mine I praised". Hero is a source of his pride, and now of his shame, and the speech is about him. When Beatrice tells him "my cousin is belied!" and that she has been Hero's bedfellow for a year, he answers with a question about men: "Would the two princes lie and Claudio lie". The word of two noble men is worth more to him than a woman's evidence. Gail Kern Paster, in an essay for the Folger Shakespeare Library, argues that Elizabethan patriarchy depended on the chastity of wives and daughters, which it could not check, because that chastity secured the legitimate passing on of land, wealth, rank and name. She adds that in the drama of the period many more wives are falsely accused than are guilty. Read this way, Leonato's "mine" is the voice of a father whose name and heirs depend on his daughter's reputation, and the men's readiness to believe the worst is a fear built into the system. That is one reading, but it explains why Hero cannot defend herself by speaking.

Beatrice is the best contrast. In Act 2 she answers Leonato's hope of a husband with "Would it not grieve a woman to be overmastered with a piece of valiant dust?" She is funny and she says what she thinks, and her uncle lets her. In the same scene, though, Leonato tells Hero "you know your answer" if the Prince asks for her hand, so the two cousins are not treated alike. Hero is told what to say, and Beatrice is allowed to joke because she is not the one being married off.

Beatrice's anger after the rejection shows both her power and its limit. She can name the wrong: "Sweet Hero, she is wronged, she is slandered, she is undone." But she cannot act on it. "O, that I were a man for his sake!" is her complaint, and "I cannot be a man with wishing; therefore I will die a woman with grieving" says she has no way to do what she wants. She tells Benedick "Kill Claudio", so her only route to action is through a man. The irony is that she says men "are only turned into tongue", while it is her own tongue that is the strongest thing on stage.

The ending gives the women more control, but not all of it. Hero returns with "One Hero died defiled, but I do live, / And surely as I live, I am a maid", and the line shows that she can speak when the plot lets her. Yet the plan to hide her came from others, and Claudio only accepts her after Leonato makes him "take her hand" and swear. Beatrice gives in to Benedick "upon great persuasion", and he ends the argument with "Peace! I will stop your mouth." The kiss is a joke between them, but it is also the play's last word on whether a woman's argument will be heard.

In conclusion, Hero is powerless in the extract because the men decide what she is and what her body means, and Shakespeare lets the audience see how unjust that is. Beatrice shows that women can have wit and opinion, but the play still sends her power through speech and through a man's actions. I would say Shakespeare presents women as powerless to a large degree in how they are treated, while giving them more power in how they speak.

## Notes (my reading)

- AO1: one argument (Hero's powerlessness comes from how the men treat her, and Beatrice has speech but not action), tied to the extract and to scenes in Acts 2, 4 and 5.
- AO2: the language of giving and payment ("gift", "worth", "counterpoise"), "rotten orange", the reversed blush, Leonato's repeated "mine", Beatrice's "man" lines and the ending's "stop your mouth" are analysed as methods.
- AO3: the context is Paster's argument (Folger essay) that Elizabethan patriarchy relied on unverifiable female chastity to secure inheritance, and that the drama of the time often shows wives falsely accused. It is used to explain Leonato's "mine" and why the men believe Claudio, as one reading. It is not a claim about all women or all fathers of the period. No single context or topic is required.
- AO4 (4 marks): spelling, punctuation and sentence variety. About 890 words, longer than a typical timed answer. Sample, not target.
- Limits: the Paster quotations were read on the Folger page as fetched on 26 Nov 2026. All non-extract play quotations were checked against the Folger text only. Hero's reply "Is my lord well that he doth speak so wide?" and Beatrice's lines are from the Folger text, not the printed extract. The answer does not discuss Margaret or Ursula, which is a choice of focus.
