# Question 6 (30 marks + AO4 4 marks): Julius Caesar

**Question:** see the official paper for the full wording (linked above).

## Model answer

Shakespeare presents loyalty as something that can be real and deep, and also as something that can be used. Antony's loyalty to Caesar is shown as true in this speech, but the play goes on to show how a loyal man can turn his grief into a weapon, and how a loyalty to Rome can ask a man to harm his friend.

In the extract, Antony speaks to a dead man and not to the conspirators: "O mighty Caesar! Dost thou lie so low?" The direct address and the rhetorical question set "mighty" against "low", so the first thing we hear is the distance between what Caesar was and what he is now. The list "conquests, glories, triumphs, spoils" makes his career sound large, and the verb "Shrunk" with "this little measure" makes it small. Antony is grieving a loss of greatness as well as a friend.

He then turns to the men who killed Caesar and offers himself to them: "If I myself, there is no hour so fit / As Caesar's death's hour". He asks them to "Fulfil your pleasure" now, "whilst your purpled hands do reek and smoke", and says "I shall not find myself so apt to die". The picture of the hands is vivid and brutal, and it shows that he is not afraid. The loyalty is a wish to die beside Caesar. This also fits something Brutus says earlier, when he dismisses Cassius's fear of Antony: "If he love Caesar, all that he can do / Is to himself: take thought and die for Caesar." Brutus sees the loyalty correctly, but he thinks it is harmless, and that is his mistake.

The speech is also a performance in front of armed men. Antony calls them "gentlemen" and "The choice and master spirits of this age". That is flattery, and a reader may take it as caution. Shakespeare lets us check which it is. Left alone with the body, Antony says "O pardon me, thou bleeding piece of earth, / That I am meek and gentle with these butchers." The word "butchers" shows what he really thinks, and the apology shows that his loyalty is real and that the politeness was a mask.

At the funeral, Antony turns loyalty into a tool. The "pulpit" he speaks from is an Elizabethan, not a Roman, place of public address (Bell Shakespeare), so a London audience may have found the scene close to home: a public speaker can move a whole crowd. "Friends, Romans, countrymen, lend me your ears. / I come to bury Caesar, not to praise him." He says he will not praise, and then praises. He gives his own link to Caesar, "He was my friend, faithful and just to me", and repeats "Brutus is an honorable man" until the phrase sounds like an insult. The crowd answers with "Revenge! About! Seek! Burn! Fire! Kill! / Slay! Let not a traitor live!" The list of short commands shows how fast the feeling spreads, and Antony's quiet "Now let it work. Mischief, thou art afoot" shows that he meant it to. The plebeians' own loyalty is shallow. Earlier a voice in the crowd says "We are blest that Rome is rid of him", and soon after, they cry "Never, never!" at the thought of another Caesar. They are loyal to whoever spoke last.

Brutus gives the opposite case. Bell Shakespeare's account of the play reads the first half as asking whether unsatisfied subjects should overthrow a tyrannous leader, and the second half as showing that violent usurpation is not the answer. That fits Brutus: loyalty to Rome seems to him to demand Caesar's death, and the war that follows shows the cost. His loyalty is to Rome, and he says so in a balanced sentence: "not that I loved Caesar less, but that I loved / Rome more." The structure sets friendship against country and picks country. He is sincere, since he adds "As Caesar loved me, I weep for him", and then "as he was ambitious, I slew him." The line shows that his loyalty to Rome has cost him a friend. The play does not say he was wrong, but it shows that he could not see what his choice would set loose. Antony's earlier warning of "Domestic fury and fierce civil strife" and "Cry 'Havoc!' and let slip the dogs of war" is the answer to Brutus's calm.

Loyalty to a person is also shown in smaller scenes. Calphurnia kneels to Caesar and says "Do not go forth today", and her fear is for him alone. Caesar does not listen to her. At the end, the friendship of Brutus and Cassius is tested in their quarrel, and Cassius says "Brutus hath rived my heart". When Cassius dies, Brutus calls him "The last of all the Romans", which treats the man's loyalty as a kind of greatness.

In conclusion, Shakespeare presents loyalty as strong but unstable. Antony's grief for Caesar is sincere, yet he uses it to bring the crowd to violence. Brutus is loyal to Rome, and it costs him his friend and, in the end, his life. The play suggests that loyalty is a good until it is put to use by someone who wants to win.

## Notes (my reading)

- AO1: one argument (loyalty is real but can be turned into a tool) tied to the extract and to several scenes across the play, shown in the answer text.
- AO2: direct address, the "mighty" and "low" contrast, the list, the "butchers" aside, repetition of "honorable man", the crowd's list of commands and Brutus's balanced sentence are analysed as methods.
- AO3: the context is Bell Shakespeare's historical background: the play's reading of rebellion against a tyrannous ruler and its cost, and the Elizabethan "pulpit". It is tied to Brutus's choice and to the funeral scene, and stated as a reading, not as Shakespeare's proven intention. Plutarch (North's translation) is named there as the main source. No single context or topic is required.
- AO4 (4 marks): spelling, punctuation and sentence variety. About 880 words, longer than a typical timed answer. Sample, not target.
- Limits: the Bell Shakespeare points were read on their page as fetched on 26 Nov 2026. All non-extract play quotations were checked against the Folger text only. The speaker of "We are blest that Rome is rid of him" is not named in the Folger text (a plebeian). The answer does not discuss Cassius's view of the crowd in detail, which is a choice of focus.
