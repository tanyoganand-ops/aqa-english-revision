# Question 12 (30 marks): Pride and Prejudice

**Question:** see the official paper for the full wording (linked above).

### Model answer

Austen presents Elizabeth as someone who changes her mind about the people who matter most to her, but who does not change her character. The extract shows the end of that change, and the novel shows the cost of reaching it.

In the extract, Elizabeth has already revised her view of Darcy, and Austen shows it by what she does not say. When Darcy asks her to say "at once" whether her feelings have changed, she is "too much embarrassed to say a word", and then she speaks "not very fluently". The broken speech is new for a woman who is quick and playful elsewhere. She cannot look at him: "Had Elizabeth been able to encounter his eye, she might have seen how well the expression of heartfelt delight... became him". The conditional shows that the reader sees what she cannot, and that she is now someone who feels more than she can control. Yet some things have not changed. Her reply to the story of Lady Catherine is "Yes, you know enough of my frankness to believe me capable of that", and she laughs at her own earlier rudeness: "After abusing you so abominably to your face, I could have no scruple in abusing you to all your relations." The adverb "abominably" is a joke at her own expense, and it shows that growth has not made her timid. The narrator adds an irony: Lady Catherine hoped to prevent the match, but "its effect had been exactly contrariwise", so the one person who tried to fix Elizabeth's views is the reason Darcy says "It taught me to hope".

At the start of the novel, Elizabeth's judgements are quick and sure. She has "a lively, playful disposition, which delighted in anything ridiculous", and she is ready to laugh at Darcy after hearing his remark that she is "not handsome enough to tempt me". The town agrees: he is "proud, to be above his company, and above being pleased". Wickham seems to confirm all this, so she accepts his story without evidence. Austen is setting up a character who trusts her own discernment.

The turn comes in Chapter 36, when Darcy's letter forces her to re-read what she thought she knew. She "grew absolutely ashamed of herself", and she thinks of "neither Darcy nor Wickham" without feeling "she had been blind, partial, prejudiced, absurd". The list of four short adjectives works like a verdict. Then comes her cry, "I, who have prided myself on my discernment!", and the sentence that sums up the change: "Till this moment, I never knew myself." The key word is "myself": the change in her view of others is also a change in her view of herself. She names her fault precisely: "Pleased with the preference of one, and offended by the neglect of the other... I have courted prepossession and ignorance, and driven reason away". The novel's title is answered here, since she sees that her own judgement was prejudiced as well as Darcy's pride.

The novel does not make every change complete. Elizabeth's view of Charlotte moves the other way: she "could not have supposed it possible that when called into action, she would have sacrificed every better feeling to worldly advantage". That change is a loss, not growth. Austen's narrator explains Charlotte's reasoning: marriage was "the only honourable provision for well-educated young women of small fortune" and "their pleasantest preservative from want", and Charlotte tells Elizabeth, "I ask only a comfortable home". So the novel itself ties Charlotte's choice to her financial security, and this helps the reader understand it even where Elizabeth cannot accept it. Her view of Lady Catherine and Mr Collins stays steady, which is part of why the reader trusts her frankness.

Darcy's two proposals show how much has changed. In the first, his "In vain have I struggled. It will not do" is met by an angry refusal in which she says he would have spared her concern "had you behaved in a more gentleman-like manner". In the extract the tone is open and equal. The structure of the novel, with the refusal in the middle and the acceptance near the end, gives her a clear arc from rejection to understanding.

In conclusion, Austen presents Elizabeth as a character who changes her views of Darcy, Wickham and Charlotte, but not her honesty. The change is shown through her speech and her shame, and it matters because it teaches her to see herself.
