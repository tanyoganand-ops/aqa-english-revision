## Model answer

Odimba presents Princess's dreams as the way a ten-year-old makes sense of a world that has not made much room for her. The dream is first shown as a game she controls, and then as a hope she is told she may not be allowed to have. I will argue that Odimba treats the dreams as important to Princess by giving them the opening of the play, a private space on stage, and a violent ending when the real world breaks into them.

The play begins inside the dream. The stage opens like a doll's house, with Princess standing in her cupboard room with her eyes closed, and the audience enters her imagination before the family's Christmas Day unfolds. A voice-over announces her as the winner of the Weston-super-Mare Beauties of the West Contest, and Princess gives her acceptance speech: "I want to thank my mummy, my friends, Margot and Junior..." (p. 6). The speech shows what she values most. She thanks the people she loves, which suggests that winning matters to her partly because it would make them proud. Odimba writes the language like a rehearsed performance, so Princess is both the child and the beauty queen she plays. She also introduces herself: "My name is Phyllis Princess James" (p. 6). Giving her full name to an imaginary crowd suggests that she wants to be seen and recognised, and the importance of the dream is already clear before the family scene begins.

The setting makes the dream concrete. The cupboard room is a recurring image, and it is the place where Princess plays at competing in a beauty contest. A cupboard is small and enclosed, so it can be read as a refuge, but it can also suggest how little space she has. That is one reading, not the only one. Because the dream has a place on the stage, the audience can see what is at risk later. The contrast with the real room outside is sharp. Mavis is unhappy that Princess is not listening, and her threats are casual: she takes a man's leather belt from a cupboard and flexes it. Princess has to leave the stage of her imagination for a home where discipline is frequent and quickly obeyed. The dream is important because it is the one place where she is in charge.

Odimba also shows that the dream is tied to how Princess sees herself. At one point Princess wishes her eyes were green or blue. A wish for a different colour of eyes suggests that she has begun to think that the beauty she dreams about belongs to someone who looks different from her, and it explains why winning a contest could seem so important. The play is set in Bristol in 1963, according to its publisher's description, at a time when Black British civil rights campaigners were taking to the streets. Princess's dream can therefore be read as a hope to be seen as beautiful in a society that has not always said so to Black girls. I treat this as context from the publisher's description, not as proof of what Odimba intended, but it makes the dream more than a game.

The dream is tested by other people's attitudes. In Act Two, Scene Three, Lorna defines herself with the words "Half of everything/Half-sister/Half-caste" and defines Princess as "Black". Princess tries to take Lorna's hand and Lorna "pulls away hard". The word "hard" shows how forcefully Lorna wants to separate herself, and the scene shows Princess meeting rejection from a sister she has welcomed warmly. Princess has been happy to have Lorna as her sister and wants her to stay forever. A girl who dreams of being admired is rejected by someone she wanted to love. Odimba lets the audience see how the world outside her cupboard answers her dream.

The strongest evidence of the dream's importance comes at the end of Act Two, Scene Five. In the study guide's words, Princess destroys "her world of pageantry" in the cupboard (that phrase is the guide's commentary, not a line of the play), and the stage direction reads: "She picks up costumes/dresses from her box and starts to cut them up. She kicks and screams." Just after her father leaves, she says "I am not a baby!" There are two ways to read this. One is that she wants to be treated as a grown-up. The other, as the guide puts it, is that her magical world, which in its phrase "doesn't come alive", mirrors the innocence taken from her by her father's return and by the racist attitudes she has to face. Both fit the language of the stage direction. The verbs "cut", "kicks" and "screams" show anger, and what she cuts up are the costumes from her box, the props of her pageant world. The dream is important enough that destroying it is the way she shows pain.

Odimba's structure supports this. The play opens in the dream and shows Princess destroying it, so the arc of her dreaming gives the arc of her growing up. The first scene lets her speak her hopes aloud to an imagined audience, and the later scene shows her angry and cutting up the costumes. The change is a loss, but I would not call it simple defeat. Princess is still the character the play is named for, and her anger shows that she knows what she has lost. In that sense the dream remains important even when it is gone.

In conclusion, Odimba presents Princess's dreams as important by opening the play inside her imagination, by giving the dream a place and a language, by showing how it connects to her hopes of belonging and being seen, and by showing the pain when it is attacked. The destruction of her costumes is the clearest sign that for Princess the dream was never just play.

## Notes (my reading, not an examiner result)

- AO1: the opening dream, the cupboard, the eye-colour wish, Lorna's rejection and the destruction scene, all from the guide's extract. I did not have a full scene-by-scene account, so I make no claim about events outside those moments.

- AO3: context from the publisher page (1963 Bristol; civil rights campaigners) and the guide (corporal punishment; the themes of race and prejudice). Hedged as secondary sources. I do not claim AO3 is met by ideas alone.
