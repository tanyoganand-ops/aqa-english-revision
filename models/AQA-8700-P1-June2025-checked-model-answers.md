# Question 2 (8 marks, AO2 language), lines 9 to 18

**Question:** see the official paper for the full wording (linked above).

### Model answer

Wyndham opens the morning with a metaphor that turns the sky into dirty washing-up water and the clouds into untidy rags (line 9). The adjective "slatternly", which means dirty or untidy, and the domestic image of "dishwater" make the clouds look sodden and neglected. The participle "filtering" and the adverb "weakly" (line 10) suggest that even the light struggles to reach the village, so the reader senses that something is wrong before anything is explained.

The writer then sets Midwich against Oppley. In Oppley the cocks crow and the other birds greet the dawn (lines 10 to 11). The alliteration of the crowing and the comparative adverb "melodiously" make an ordinary, pleasing morning. Then the connective "however" turns the tone, and the next clause says that in Midwich there was no birdsong (line 11). The short, blunt clause makes the absence of song feel unnatural. The repeated naming of the village at the start of the contrasting sentences (see line 4 and the following lines) keeps returning to Midwich as if it is separated from the world around it, and the clocks that simply rattle on until they run down (line 13) show that no one is there to silence them.

The key personification is the short sentence that says Midwich lay "entranced" (line 13). The whole village is treated as one body under a spell. "Entranced" is ambiguous. It can suggest something beautiful and harmless, but it also implies a powerful unseen force in control, which is quietly threatening. The idea continues when the village is said to sleep on while the rest of the world fills the morning with "clamour" (line 14). The noun "clamour" makes normal life noisy, which isolates the village further.

Wyndham then builds a list that runs from people and livestock down to the smallest creatures, moles and mice (lines 15 to 16). The repeated "its" and the move from people down to mice show that every living thing is affected, and the completeness is unsettling. The paragraph ends with the metaphor of a pocket of silence (line 16), which seals Midwich off like an enclosed space. The only sounds left, "rustling", "chiming" and "gurgle", are onomatopoeic and delicate, and they make the stillness more noticeable.

# Question 3 (8 marks, AO2 structure), whole source

**Question:** see the official paper for the full wording (linked above).

### Model answer

Wyndham begins with a wide view. The official-sounding opening, with its precise time and the verb "episodic" (line 1), gives a detached, report-like tone, and then comes a list of vehicles that disappear one after another: the bus, the truck, the fire engine and the police car. The sequence creates a growing mystery because each rescuer vanishes in turn, and the ellipsis that ends the sentence about Constable Gobby (lines 7 to 8) leaves the reader waiting for an answer.

The focus then shifts from the night to the morning and from the missing to the village itself. The contrast between Oppley, where birds sing and clocks are silenced, and Midwich, where no birds sing (line 11), moves the mood from puzzlement to eeriness. The reader now sees what has been lost instead of hearing about it second hand.

The writer next narrows the focus to the Post Office van, which travels towards Midwich. Time markers, such as the dawn that is still weak (line 19) and the van's arrival half an hour later (line 35), keep the account steady and factual, so that the strange events seem more disturbing. The narrative follows the van to the crash scene, where the earlier list is answered: the fire engine, the car and the man with a bicycle are all found. This gives a circular structure, but the central mystery of why they stopped is withheld.

The final focus is on one driver, who collapses quietly (line 39). The sudden collapse, after so much stillness, turns the mystery into a threat. The text ends with the surviving driver turning back towards Oppley (line 42). This returns to the opening image of the dead telephones, and leaves the reader with unanswered questions.

# Question 4 (20 marks, AO4), lines 19 to the end

**Question:** see the official paper for the full wording (linked above).

Statement (Q4): the question gives a two-part statement about how mysterious the situation is and how clearly the writer shows that; see the official paper for its wording.

### Model answer

I agree strongly that the situation is mysterious, and partly that it is hard to understand: the writer shows the reader exactly what has happened to the vehicles but refuses to say why, and that gap is the source of the mystery.

The first part of the statement is partly true. The text clearly shows the result. The van finds a fire engine half over on its side and a car that has climbed the bank (line 29). The verbs "keeled" and "climbed" suggest vehicles that have lost all control, and the man and bicycle lying in the ditch (line 31) link directly back to the missing Constable Gobby. A reader can therefore connect these crashes to the earlier list of vanished vehicles. What cannot be understood is the cause. Nobody in the extract explains why the drivers lost control.

The writer deepens that uncertainty by choosing a calm, factual voice. The van stops to ask whether Midwich has shown any signs of life, and the village is called "incommunicado" (line 23). The formal word "incommunicado" and the way the van, not the driver, is the subject of the verbs, as if the machine rather than the driver is in charge, give an official, distant tone which does not offer any opinion or explanation. This detachment makes the events more unsettling because the narrator seems as helpless as the people who are found.

The mystery becomes a threat when the first van driver is forced to swerve and his van comes to a stop in the hedge (line 34), and then the bus arrives and slots into the gap (line 35). The adverb "neatly" is darkly comic, as if the trap has been set to catch each new vehicle. Even the light is described as weak and the daylight as slow to arrive (lines 19 and 24 to 25), which makes the atmosphere uneasy.

The most striking moment is when a man, without any warning, folds up and drops to the ground (line 39). The adverb "quietly" and the verb phrase for the fold make a collapse that is sudden and silent, like a switch being turned off. The writer never says that he is hurt: the passengers' heads are described as motionless (line 41), and "motionless" is carefully neutral, neither dead nor alive. The reader is left to imagine the cause.

Overall, I agree that the writer makes the mystery clear, because every detail points to an unseen force that affects everyone who approaches. I only partly agree that it is hard to understand what happened, because the result is spelled out. It is the explanation that is deliberately denied, and that is what keeps the reader reading.

# Question 5 (40 marks: 24 AO5, 16 AO6), option chosen: a story about a day when technology stopped

(The alternative, a description of a town or village from a picture, is not used here because the picture is not reproducible in this text.)

### Model answer (559 words by count)

The first thing I noticed was that I had not been woken up.

No alarm, no buzz, no little blue glow on the ceiling. Just grey light and the sound of a neighbour's dog barking at nothing. I reached for my phone and found a black rectangle that reflected only my own face, puzzled and unwashed. I pressed every button I could find. It stayed blank.

Downstairs, Mum was standing in the kitchen with the kettle in her hand, as though it had betrayed her. "Power's off," she said. "And the Wi-Fi's dead too." It was ten past eight on a school morning, and I realised that I had never noticed how much of the world hummed until it stopped.

We walked to school because the bus did not come. Nobody told us to; there was simply nobody to ask. All along the high street, people stood outside shops with their cards in their hands, staring at tills that had gone dead. A man in a suit was shaking his phone like a snow globe. A woman at the bakery counter was saying, again and again, "I can give you a pastry, I just can't take your money." Her voice cracked slightly on the last word.

At school the clocks had stopped at 7.52. They seemed to have given up on us. Mr Okafor stood at the front of the hall without a microphone, and for the first time in my life I heard the whole year group go quiet. "We don't know what has happened," he said. "So we will do what we can do. We will use paper."

It was strange how quickly we adapted. In the first lesson we copied a map of Europe from a faded atlas, and I found that my hand remembered how to draw a coastline. At break we did not stand in our usual huddles staring down. We played football on the field, shouting, laughing, tripping over each other like we were seven years old again. A boy I had never spoken to lent me his pencil. I thanked him and he nodded as if it was nothing, and it felt like something.

By the afternoon, rumours spread through the corridors. A storm in space. A cyber attack. The end of the world. Someone said that the whole country had gone dark. Nobody knew, and for the first time that did not seem to matter as much as I expected it to.

When I got home, Mum had lit candles in the kitchen and put out a board game that had not been opened for years. The dust on the lid was thick. We played until the light left the window, and she laughed so hard at my terrible cheating that she had to put her hand over her mouth.

That night I lay in bed in a silence so complete that I could hear my own heartbeat. I was frightened, but I was also, strangely, awake in a way I could not remember. I looked at the black phone on the table and, for once, I did not want it to light up.

When the power returned the next morning the lights flickered on one by one, and the fridge shuddered back to life. Everything hummed again. I stood in the hallway listening to it, and I missed the quiet.

This is a provisional self-assessment against the published descriptors. An examiner would judge each strand holistically and could place it differently.

AO5 content and organisation (24 marks). Strengths: a consistent, controlled first-person voice that suits a story (tone, style and register); a clear structure that follows one day and returns to its opening image (the humming) at the end; some crafted language ("shaking his phone like a snow globe", the clocks that "seemed to have given up on us"); and a developing idea that community returns when technology stops, finished with a mixed feeling ("I missed the quiet"). This fits the Level 3 to Level 4 descriptors on structure, ideas and register. Limitation: the vocabulary is mostly plain and controlled, not especially wide-ranging, and the discourse markers are mostly time phrases ("By the afternoon", "That night"). So the vocabulary and linking strands sit nearer Level 3 than the top of Level 4. I would not claim the upper Level 4 range (22 to 24) for this answer.

AO6 technical accuracy (16 marks). Strengths: sentence demarcation is secure; there is a deliberate mix of sentence forms (single-sentence hook, a long list sentence, a short final sentence); punctuation includes a semicolon, speech marks and apostrophes; spelling is accurate. Limitation: the vocabulary is varied but not ambitious, which the top level looks for. The Level 4 descriptors are judged as an overall picture of accuracy and control, not as a checklist of marks of punctuation. A provisional reading is the upper part of Level 3 to Level 4.

Practice example note: the story is 559 words, a long example for study. It is not a word-count target, and it does not show what can be written in the advised 45 minutes, which includes planning and checking.
