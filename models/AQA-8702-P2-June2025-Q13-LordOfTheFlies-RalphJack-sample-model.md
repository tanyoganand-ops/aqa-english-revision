## Model answer

Golding shows Ralph and Jack moving from rivalry inside one group into open war between two. At the start they share an expedition and a common aim. By the end Jack's tribe is hunting Ralph across the island. Golding uses the change to suggest that when rules lose their force, two boys who began on the same side can become enemies. I will argue that the relationship passes through three stages: uneasy partnership, a split over what leadership means, and pursuit.

At first the two are partners with different instincts. Ralph is voted chief after he blows the conch, and the choir arrive under Jack's command, so from the first chapter Jack is a leader who has lost a vote. Golding notes how the choir are controlled: the boy in charge is dressed like the others 'though his cap badge was golden' (p. 26), a detail which marks Jack as a figure of authority even before he has power. Ralph, Jack and Simon then explore the island together, and when they find a trapped piglet Jack hesitates, fails to kill it and promises that next time it will not escape. The moment shows a boy not yet able to kill, but his promise suggests a need to prove himself, which is my inference. The conch is the symbol of Ralph's authority here, because the other boys come when it is blown. Authority rests on agreement, not force, and that is why it is fragile.

The first real division comes when Jack becomes a hunter. He camouflages his face and kills a pig, while the hunters let the signal fire go out as a ship passes. Ralph and Piggy argue with them, then watch with envy as the boys re-enact the kill in a tribal dance. Golding makes the painted face important in this early scene and returns to it much later, around the approach to Castle Rock, where the notes quote the comment that they 'understood only too well the liberation into savagery that the concealing paint brought' (p. 212). Read back from that later line, the word 'liberation' suggests why the mask appeals: it frees a boy from the self that obeyed rules. Ralph's envy matters as well, because it shows that he is not simply opposed to Jack's way of life but partly drawn to it. The fire and the hunt now divide them. One boy stands for rescue and the other for the feast.

The split follows. Jack calls an assembly with the conch, warns of the beast and calls Ralph a coward who should no longer lead. When the boys refuse to vote Ralph out, Jack leaves in tears, then declares himself chief of a new tribe: 'I'm going to be chief' (p. 165). The sentence is a contrast with Ralph's election, since Jack takes the title instead of being given it. Many of the older boys join him and his group steals fire from Ralph's. Golding then shows Jack's new style of leadership in the chapter of Simon's death, where he is 'painted and garlanded', sitting 'like an idol' (p. 183), and offers food and protection: 'my hunters will protect you from the beast' (p. 185). He leads by fear and reward, while Ralph leads by argument. The other boys decide the relationship here, because they choose which of the two to follow, and most choose Jack. The loss of the conch's power is made explicit when the conch is said not to 'count at this end of the island' (p. 186).

By the later chapters the relationship is violent. Ralph, Piggy, Sam and Eric go to Jack's end of the island to demand Piggy's glasses, and Jack argues with Ralph until the quarrel becomes a fight. Roger releases a rock that kills Piggy and breaks the conch. The two leaders are still arguing when the conch breaks, and Golding uses this to show that Ralph's way of governing has been destroyed. Ralph is hurt and runs away, and Roger asserts authority, which suggests that power has passed to someone harsher than Jack. Near the end, at Castle Rock, Golding describes the two leaders as having swapped places: 'Then they were apart once more, their positions reversed' (p. 218). The line is useful because it makes them mirror images: one now leads a tribe and the other is alone, yet they remain tied to each other.

The last chapters complete the change. Ralph is wounded and hiding, and Sam and Eric are forced to betray him. The hunters set fire to the undergrowth to drive him into the open, and he is chased to the beach, where he collapses at the feet of a naval officer. The fire, which once stood for rescue, is now used as a weapon, so the same symbol measures how far apart the boys have moved. Even then Golding keeps a link between them. Ralph thinks of 'that indefinable connection between himself and Jack; who therefore would never let him alone; never' (p. 226). The repetition of 'never' stresses that the hostility cannot end while both are alive, and the phrase 'indefinable connection' suggests that the relationship is more than simple hatred. When the boys weep at the end, Ralph weeps 'for the end of innocence, the darkness of man's heart' (p. 248), which turns a story about two rivals into a comment on all of them.

The structure of the novel supports this movement. The island begins as a place of exploration and ends as a place of fear and fire, so the slide in the relationship follows the island's slide from order into savagery. There are no adults, so nobody outside the group can settle the argument. This links to the ideas of power and control: Ralph's leadership depends on agreement and Jack's on fear and force, and Golding makes the reader see which kind of power destroys the group. Ralph is not perfect, since he is tempted by the hunters' dance, so the novel does not present a simple contest between good and evil. This is one reading only, and the novel could also be read as a story about boys growing up without adult rules.

Context adds to this reading. The Faber notes record that Golding served in the Royal Navy from 1940 to 1945 and that this gave him direct experience of the human capacity for brutality, and that he himself said those horrors lay behind his picture of the boys' behaviour. The notes also record that the novel was first published in 1954, shortly after the war, and that it suggests evil is inherent in everyone, though critics differ on how far it should be read as an allegory. I use this as one publisher-hosted source, not as proof of Golding's intention. It supports the argument that the collapse of the friendship between Ralph and Jack is not just a quarrel between two boys but a picture of how quickly order can fail, with Jack's turn to the hunt standing for the pull of violence that Golding says he had seen.

In conclusion, Golding shows Ralph and Jack moving from rivals in a vote to enemies in a hunt, and he marks each stage with a symbol: the conch, the fire, the painted face and the burning island. Their relationship changes because the other boys choose Jack and because the rules that held the group together are broken, and it ends with one boy trying to survive the other.
