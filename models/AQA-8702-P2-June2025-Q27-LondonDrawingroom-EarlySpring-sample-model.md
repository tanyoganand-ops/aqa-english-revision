# Question 27 (30 marks): In a London Drawingroom and Lines Written in Early Spring

**Question:** see the official paper (link above).

**Mark scheme:** see the official mark scheme (link above). Its indicative content is a guide only.

## Model answer

Eliot and Wordsworth both show a place shaping the way a person feels, but they show opposite effects. In Eliot's poem, the city weighs people down and makes them look away from each other. In Wordsworth's poem, a grove makes the speaker feel connected to nature, though his pleasure turns to sadness when he thinks of people.

Eliot begins with the sky and the weather, because the first thing the city does is block the light. The sky is "yellowed by the smoke", and the line of houses is "like solid fog". The simile makes the buildings feel as heavy and shapeless as the smoke. Then she writes of "Monotony of surface & of form", and the abstract nouns give the street no feature to remember. The place is dull before it is cruel. She adds that there is nothing "to hang a guess upon", so even the imagination has nothing to work with. A place can limit what a mind can do.

The second half shows the effect on people. "All hurry on & look upon the ground", and the short, flat verbs make the crowd seem mechanical. Nobody looks up, so nobody sees the sky or each other. The cabs and carriages are "closed, in multiplied identity", so even the traffic hides its passengers, and the repeated sameness suggests that people become part of the pattern. Eliot ends with a metaphor: "The world seems one huge prison-house & court". The word "seems" keeps this as an impression, but "prison-house" and "court" suggest confinement and judgment. The last line, "With lowest rate of colour, warmth & joy", lists three things the city lacks. The poem is one block of lines with no break and no regular rhyme scheme, and I read that as part of the point: there is nowhere to pause, like the street it describes.

Wordsworth begins differently. "I heard a thousand blended notes" opens with sound, and "blended" suggests that the natural world makes one harmony. The speaker is "reclined" in a grove, in a "sweet mood", and the place lets him relax. Where Eliot's people look down and rush on, he sits, listens and looks closely. He says "'tis my faith that every flower / Enjoys the air it breathes", and he sees "a thrill of pleasure" in the birds' smallest movements. The place gives him an idea, that nature is full of enjoyment, and it affects what he believes. That is a positive effect on a mind, and a stronger one than the dull view in the Eliot poem, where the mind has nothing to hold.

The ways the poets use voice are also different. Wordsworth speaks as "I" and uses the first person to say what he hears, believes and feels. Eliot looks at the scene from a window, so her speaker is at a distance, and the poem describes the crowd as "All" and "men" rather than as individuals. The first-person voice makes his reaction personal, while her more distant view makes the effect on everyone seem general. It is a small difference, but it shows that one poet is inside the place and the other is looking at it.

Wordsworth does not simply praise nature. In the first stanza, "pleasant thoughts / Bring sad thoughts to the mind", and the poem turns on the line "What man has made of man". The line comes twice, in the second stanza and at the end, and the second time it is a question: "Have I not reason to lament". The repetition and the question show that the pleasure of the place cannot keep out his sadness about people. Here the two poems meet. Eliot's city is made by people and makes them joyless, and Wordsworth fears what people have done to each other. For both poets the human world is a cause of sorrow, but only Eliot places it in a street. The regular quatrains of Wordsworth's poem, with their alternate rhymes, are calm and ordered, so the sadness at the end sits inside a peaceful form.

Context gives one more way to read the two. AQA's teaching guide to this cluster says that Eliot moved to London in 1850 and wrote the poem in 1865, and describes it as criticising the city while showing sympathy for its people. It says Wordsworth's poem was published in 1798 and calls him a Romantic poet. I use these only as background and I do not claim that either poet had one intention. They suggest that Eliot is writing about the city she lived in and Wordsworth about nature as a source of feeling, which fits the contrast I have drawn.

In conclusion, both poets show that places affect people's lives, but in different ways. Eliot's smoky street makes people hurry, look down and feel confined, and the effect is mostly negative. Wordsworth's grove gives the speaker pleasure and a sense of belonging, but he still ends in sadness about people. The comparison shows that a place can change how a person feels, though the biggest sorrow in both poems comes from other people.
