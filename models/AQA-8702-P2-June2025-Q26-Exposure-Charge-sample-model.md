# Question 26 (30 marks)

**Question:** Poetry, Question 26 (Section B, Power and conflict). See the official paper (linked above) for the full wording.

### Model answer

Owen and Tennyson both write about soldiers who endure, but they disagree about what the endurance means. Owen's soldiers are trapped by weather and waiting, and the poem sees no glory in it. Tennyson's cavalry ride into the guns, and the poem asks the reader to honour them. I will compare the two poems' treatment of the soldiers' experience, their use of repetition, and their attitudes to duty.

Both poems present conflict as something a group goes through, but they put different enemies in front of the group. In 'Exposure' the main enemy is the weather. "the merciless iced east winds that knive us" turns wind into a weapon, and the verb "knive" is violent and unusual, as if the air attacks them with blades. Dawn is described as "massing in the east her melancholy army", so even the sky is an army, and the soldiers are the ones under attack. In 'The Charge of the Light Brigade' the enemy is human and obvious, "Cannon to right of them, / Cannon to left of them, / Cannon in front of them". The repeated structure surrounds the men, so that the reader feels the trap closing, much as Owen's repetition suggests a trap. The difference is that Tennyson's enemy can be fought: "Flashed all their sabres bare". Owen's cannot, so his soldiers can only "cringe in holes".

The two poets also use repetition differently. Owen's refrain "But nothing happens" closes four of his stanzas and, in the last, is made worse by the closing line, "All their eyes are ice, / But nothing happens". The words get more hopeless each time, because the first time they suggest boredom and the last time they follow death. The refrain is flat and short, and it imitates the endless, useless waiting. Tennyson's repetition drives forward. "Half a league, half a league, / Half a league onward" copies the pace of horses, and the refrain "Rode the six hundred" makes the group a single unit, almost one body. Owen's repetition stops the poem from moving, while Tennyson's pushes it on.

Duty is the biggest difference. Tennyson admires obedience: "Theirs not to make reply, / Theirs not to reason why, / Theirs but to do and die". The parallel structure and the strong rhythm make the three lines sound like a rule that cannot be questioned, and the soldiers are praised for following it. Yet the line before it, "Someone had blundered", admits that the order was a mistake, so the poem contains a quiet criticism of the leaders even as it praises the men. Owen's soldiers ask a question that Tennyson's never do: "What are we doing here?" The question ends the second stanza and nobody answers it. By the end the soldiers are dying for a reason they cannot name. According to the Wilfred Owen Association, Owen's dream of home echoes a popular wartime song about keeping the home fires burning: his fires are almost dead, and the "kind fires" he mentions are out of reach. He says "we believe not otherwise can kind fires burn", which shows them clinging to a belief that their suffering has a purpose, but "our love is made afraid" suggests the belief is thin. Tennyson gives the soldiers a purpose, but Owen leaves them unsure of one.

The poets' circumstances help explain the difference. Tennyson wrote his poem in 1854 after reading an account of the charge at Balaclava in the Crimean War, and it made him "the nation's poet" and shaped how people felt about that war. 'Exposure' draws on Owen's own experience of the front in the winter of 1917, which was exceptionally cold, with the armies locked in stalemate. The poem was shaped later: the Wilfred Owen Association says its final version belongs to September 1918, and I have not established where or when the earlier drafts were written. He also wrote that his subject was "the pity of War" and that his book was not about heroes. So each poem matches its writer's position: Tennyson celebrates from a distance, and Owen reports from inside the experience, even though he shaped the poem later. The line "Someone had blundered" was dropped from a revised version published in 1855, after critics objected to rhyming "blundered" with "hundred", but it belongs to the poem as first published and as printed in the text I used.

The endings show the contrast most clearly. Tennyson finishes with a command to the reader: "Honour the charge they made! / Honour the Light Brigade, / Noble six hundred!" The imperative and the adjective "Noble" turn the survivors and the dead into a monument. The last stanza of 'Exposure' offers no monument, only "The burying-party, picks and shovels in shaking grasp", pausing "over half-known faces". "Half-known" suggests that war has taken even the dead men's identities. The poems share an interest in the ordinary soldier, but one ends with praise and the other with a grave.

In conclusion, both poets show soldiers as a group who suffer together and who suffer because of decisions made elsewhere. Tennyson turns that suffering into honour, and Owen shows it as waiting, cold and loss, with nothing to honour. The comparison is clearest in their repetition: one drives the soldiers forward, and the other keeps them in place.

### Notes (my reading, not an examiner result)

- AO1: one comparative line (what the group faces, how repetition shapes it, what duty means, how each poem ends). Both poems are compared in the four body paragraphs after the introduction, and the conclusion compares them again.
- AO2: "knive", the army image for dawn, the repeated cannon structure, the two refrains, the parallel "Theirs" lines, imperative and "Noble", "half-known". Sound and rhythm are mentioned for Tennyson but not analysed in depth.
- AO3: patriotism and duty, and power and powerlessness, are covered through the "Someone had blundered" and "What are we doing here?" contrast. Context is used to explain a difference in attitude (a poem written at a distance from the battle, and one drawn from front-line experience and finished later), not as a list of facts. Conflict with the natural world is covered in paragraph two through the weather, but not as a context point.
