# Question 4 (30 marks + AO4 4 marks): The Merchant of Venice

**Question:** see the official paper for the full wording (linked above).

### Model answer

Shakespeare presents wealth as something that can be loved, used or distrusted, and the characters are judged by what they do with it. In the extract Shylock's attitude is the most extreme, but the rest of the play sets it beside very different views.

In the conversation with Tubal, Shylock talks about his daughter and his money in the same breath, and money wins. His first question is about Jessica, but the next thing he does is count his losses: "A diamond gone cost me two thousand ducats in Frankfurt!" The exclamation mark and the exact sum show how closely he tracks his money, and the repetition in "precious, precious jewels" makes them sound like a loved thing. Then he says "I would my daughter were dead at my foot, and the jewels in her ear: would she were hearsed at my foot, and the ducats in her coffin." The second wish copies the first, and the jewels and ducats follow his daughter into her coffin, so the money is no less present than the girl. The cruelty of this is that his daughter has become a thing connected to his loss. He also lists his troubles as "loss upon loss" and "no satisfaction, no revenge", and the short repeated "no" phrases make his grief sound like a ledger. When Tubal mentions Antonio's ruin, he cries "I thank God, I thank God", and the repeated thanks show how quickly his misery turns to joy when he thinks of revenge. The play reports the same mix in Act 2, where Solanio quotes his cry in the street: "My daughter, O my ducats, O my daughter!" The words alternate, so daughter and ducats are placed side by side.

Shylock's attitude to money is also shaped by how Venice treats him. He tells Antonio "You call me misbeliever, cutthroat dog, / And spet upon my Jewish gaberdine, / And all for use of that which is mine own", so the insults are tied to his moneylending. Earlier he says Antonio "lends out money gratis and brings down / The rate of usance here with us in Venice", so Antonio's free lending is a threat to his trade. He defends what he calls his "well-won thrift". When the Duke's sentence takes half his wealth, he answers "Nay, take my life and all" and says "you take my life / When you do take the means whereby I live", and the line shows that to him money is survival as well as profit. A reader can see why he values it, even if the play does not defend his choice to put it above his daughter.

Other characters hold other attitudes. Antonio is generous with money: he tells Bassanio "My purse, my person, my extremest means / Lie all unlocked to your occasions". The word "unlocked" is the opposite of Shylock's hoarding. Yet the generosity has a cost, because it puts Antonio in the bond that nearly kills him. Jessica uses wealth to escape: "I will make fast the doors and gild myself / With some more ducats". She steals from her father to leave his house, so her attitude to wealth is a means to freedom, though it also confirms Shylock's fear of the Christians.

In Belmont, attitudes to wealth are tested by the caskets. Bassanio rejects the gold casket with "Therefore, then, thou gaudy gold, / Hard food for Midas, I will none of thee" and says "The world is still deceived with ornament." The adjective "gaudy" and the myth of Midas, whose touch turned his food to gold, make wealth look like something that could starve a person. Portia then hands over her wealth as love: "Myself, and what is mine, to you and yours / Is now converted", with "This house, these servants, and this same myself / Are yours". The list moves from her property to herself, so wealth and relationship are joined. This contrasts with Venice, where money creates debt and bonds.

For an Elizabethan audience, the context explains why wealth was a loaded subject. The historian Stephen Alford writes that attitudes to usury and money-lending in Elizabethan England were complicated, and that "interest" was a concept that many people found as offensive as usury. He reads Shylock and Antonio as "two extreme positions on money" (Folger Shakespeare Library excerpt). I use this as one reading of how the play sets two attitudes against each other. It should not be taken as a fact about Jewish or Christian people. The play itself shows Christians insulting Shylock while needing his money, and his reasoning on revenge, "The villainy you teach me I will execute", suggests that the play also asks how the treatment he receives shapes how he behaves with money.

In conclusion, Shakespeare presents wealth as tied up with religion, power and love. Shylock treasures money in a city that insults him and still needs it, Antonio lends freely but risks his life for it, Jessica takes ducats to escape, and Bassanio and Portia treat wealth as something to give away for love. The play offers no single verdict, but it makes the audience ask who is called greedy, and why.
