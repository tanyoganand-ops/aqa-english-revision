# Question 11 (30 marks): Frankenstein

**Question:** see the official paper for the full wording (linked above).

## Model answer

Shelley presents family as the thing that shapes a person, for good and for harm. The extract shows a family that works, and the rest of the novel shows what happens when a father fails his child.

In the extract, the family is a place of care that is also a kind of control. Victor's father "strove to shelter her, as a fair exotic is sheltered by the gardener, from every rougher wind". The gardener image makes his mother a delicate plant and his father the one who protects her. It is loving, and it also treats her as something to be managed. The religious words "gratitude and worship" and "reverence" make the love close to prayer. When Victor says "I was their plaything and their idol, and something better – their child", the list rises from a toy to an idol to a child, and the last word is the one that carries a duty. His parents know it: his "future lot" was "in their hands to direct to happiness or misery, according as they fulfilled their duties towards me". The phrase "guided by a silken cord" suggests gentle control that the child hardly feels.

That passage matters because Victor later becomes a parent and fails. When he plans his creation he dreams that "No father could claim the gratitude of his child so completely as I should deserve theirs." The word "father" is the same word he has just used for his own, and it shows that he has learned to want a child's gratitude, but not to give a child's care. After the creature wakes he runs away. The ideal in Chapter 1, in which a parent accepts "duties" towards a helpless being, is the standard that Victor breaks.

The novel also shows that love in a family can be possessive. Elizabeth is called "my more than sister", but his mother gives her to him as "a pretty present", and he takes the words literally: "mine to protect, love, and cherish". It is a warm sentence, and "mine" is also the word of ownership. When he is making the creature, he puts his family aside: "I wished, as it were, to procrastinate all that related to my feelings of affection until the great object... should be completed." His family is something he feels he can come back to later, and the delay costs him.

The creature has no family, and Shelley makes the lack the cause of his anger. He tells Victor "I ought to be thy Adam; but I am rather the fallen angel, whom thou drivest from joy for no misdeed." The Bible comparison makes Victor a creator who owes his creature love. He also says "I was benevolent and good; misery made me a fiend", which says that his character was formed by how he was treated. His demand "Do your duty towards me, and I will do mine towards you" echoes the extract's word "duties" and turns it against Victor. Nurture, and not nature, is the point.

The De Lacey family shows the creature what he lacks. He calls the cottagers "my protectors" and, when they leave, says they "had broken the only link that held me to the world". He watches a family that loves, and he is shut out of it. His request to Victor is for a family of his own: "I am alone, and miserable; man will not associate with me; but one as deformed and horrible as myself would not deny herself to me." Later he adds that two monsters "shall be more attached to one another". His wish is not for power, but for a companion.

Shelley's own life gives a way to read this. The British Library biography of Mary Shelley notes that her mother, Mary Wollstonecraft, died days after Shelley's birth in 1797, so Shelley grew up without her. I read Victor's idealised parents in Chapter 1 against that background, as a picture of a family that an author who never knew her mother might imagine. This is a tentative reading. The novel does not prove it, and I make no claim about what Shelley intended.

In conclusion, Shelley presents family as the source of love, duty and identity. Victor is raised as a loved child, but he does not pass that on, and the creature, who is given nothing, suffers from the same failure. By the end, both are alone, and the novel suggests that this is what happens when the duty at the heart of a family is ignored.

## Notes (my reading)

- AO1: one argument (family is the source of care and duty, and the failure of that duty drives the tragedy), tied to the extract and to Victor's family, the creature and the De Laceys.
- AO2: the gardener image, the religious words, the rising list, "silken cord", "mine", the Adam allusion and the echo of "duties" are analysed as methods.
- AO3: the context is one biographical fact, from the British Library biography of Mary Shelley (her mother died days after her birth in 1797). Other sources give the exact interval differently, so the answer says "days". It is used only for one tentative reading of the idealised family in Chapter 1, and it is not a claim about Shelley's intention. No single context or topic is required.
