## Provisional study response

Russell shows class as a large, lasting and unfair influence on the characters. Because Mickey and Edward are twins, class is almost the only thing that separates them, so the play can show what it does to two lives that began as one. I will argue that Russell presents class as a force that shapes opportunity, money, speech and even fear, and that he invites the audience to ask whether class, or superstition, is to blame for the tragedy.

Russell makes class visible before the story begins. The Narrator's prologue asks the audience to judge for themselves how Mrs Johnstone came to play her part, so the play opens by asking for a verdict. The stage directions present her as a woman who looks much older than her years, and her opening song counts the cost in children and hunger. Poverty shows in her body, and the dance-hall tune about a film star sets glamour against that hardship.

Money controls even food. A milkman refuses to deliver without payment, and the children's voices off stage show the effects: one is embarrassed by free school dinners, and another complains that the house never has enough to eat. Mrs Johnstone adds that the Welfare have already been in touch and that love is not enough to feed a family. Her poverty brings official scrutiny as well as hunger, and she loves her children without being able to feed them, so love does not protect her from class.

Mrs Lyons has money and meets the same news very differently. She describes her large house as too big for her, because it was bought for children who never came. When Mrs Johnstone says that with twins she cannot cope, the Narrator remarks that an idea once planted can quickly take root and become a plan, and Mrs Lyons, holding back her excitement, asks for one of the babies. Mrs Johnstone's crisis becomes Mrs Lyons's chance, and the reader sees that wealth gives the power to turn another woman's need into an offer. An earlier scene about new shoes on a table sets the other explanation in play: Mrs Johnstone insists she is not superstitious while the Narrator lists omens, and Mrs Lyons laughs at her. That is the first hint of the question the play ends on, whether class or superstition shapes the brothers' fate.

The same choice is described by the exam boards, and it is the choice that begins the plot. WJEC describes Mrs Johnstone as a working-class mother of seven who cleans for Mrs Lyons and is expecting twins, and Mrs Lyons as a wealthy, middle-class woman desperate for a child. Mrs Lyons persuades Mrs Johnstone to give one twin away. The WJEC exemplar student response quotes Mrs Lyons promising that a child given to her "wouldn't have to worry where his next meal was coming from" and would have a "bike with both wheels on". Those phrases speak to the poverty Mrs Johnstone already knows. The promise works because Mrs Johnstone's life is hard, and WJEC says she can be seen as a victim of poverty, as well as desperate, foolish or naive. I read the scene as showing that money gives one mother the power to make an offer and leaves the other with little power to refuse.

The effects of class are not the same for both mothers, and that is part of Russell's point. The WJEC exemplar response describes Mrs Lyons's home as a "palace", yet it says she feels alone, because her husband is often away on business, she cannot have children, and he refuses to adopt. WJEC also says she becomes paranoid that her son belongs more to his birth mother. The OCR guide makes a similar point about money, saying that Mrs Johnstone's life of debt leads to problems, but that Mrs Lyons's wealthy existence does not bring her contentment either. Wealth in the play therefore gives power, but it does not give peace.

The Act 2 kitchen extract printed by WJEC shows both women under pressure. The WJEC exemplar response says Mrs Lyons is desperate to be rid of Mrs Johnstone and links this to the repetition of "thousands". In the extract, the offer is blunt: "Is it money you want? ... I'll get it for you. If you move away from here." The reply is a reminder of the earlier bribe, "You bought me off once before", and a refusal from Mrs Johnstone: "I don't want your money. I've made a life out here. It's not much of one maybe, but I made it." The offer treats a person as something that can be paid to go away, which suggests how Mrs Lyons uses her class position. The refusal, with its modest "not much of one maybe", shows dignity and a sense of a life that, however poor, belongs to Mrs Johnstone. Mrs Lyons's own admission in the extract, "But I never made him mine", shows that money and position could not give her what she wanted.

Education and work then divide the brothers. CCEA says the difference between working class and middle class is reflected in the comprehensive schooling of Mickey and the public schooling of Edward. WJEC says that Edward is articulate, polite and well mannered, educated and wealthy, and becomes a councillor, while Mickey hates his factory job, is later unemployed, turns to crime and goes to prison, where he becomes depressed and addicted to medication. OCR reads this as Russell showing that wealth brings different educational opportunities and that, without a better education, Mickey has few options, so that his mistakes are to be seen sympathetically. A candidate could also argue that Mickey is responsible for his own choices, but the parallel lives, one rising and one falling, make class look like a pattern, not a coincidence. WJEC adds that Mr Lyons dictates Mickey's letter of dismissal, so the family with more power decides the working family's job.

Russell also shows class through speech. CCEA says the dialogue is written in a Liverpool dialect and that class distinction is inferred through the language, particularly between Edward and Mickey, giving the 'F' word and 'a dictionary' as examples. They suggest that the boys have been taught to speak in different registers, so the audience can hear class before it is explained. CCEA adds that standard, received English is sometimes misunderstood in the play, which creates tension or confusion. Because language reveals class, even a simple conversation shows the gap between the boys.

The play also shows class in everyday money. WJEC's context panel says that Mrs Johnstone is visited by "various debt collectors" because she has been living on the "never never", and that Mickey's unemployment is "just another sign of the times". WJEC offers these phrases as evidence of the financial difficulties of the 1980s. CCEA also says that Liverpool had high unemployment and that the play reflects a large disparity between the classes. This background suggests that the play's picture of debt and unemployment would have been recognisable to its early audiences.

Russell also uses structure to make class feel like fate. CCEA says the play opens with a flashback to the final scene as a prologue that predicts events, and that foreshadowing and dramatic irony warn the audience of what is to come. WJEC adds that the Narrator asks the audience to judge events for themselves and, at the end, asks whether superstition or class is to blame. WJEC's context panel quotes the superstition that "if either twin learns that he once was a pair, they shall both immediately die". OCR argues that the use of twins persuades the audience that attitudes in society influence people's lives more than their own efforts, and says it sees the play as an objection to the view of Margaret Thatcher's government that anyone who wanted to work hard could succeed. The Narrator's closing question keeps the play open, because it places class beside superstition. But the pairing invites the audience to take class seriously as a cause.

The ending shows the cost. WJEC says Linda and Edward begin a "light romance" which Mrs Lyons points out to Mickey, who then takes a gun and confronts Edward. Mrs Johnstone tells them they are twins, Mickey shoots Edward and the police then shoot Mickey. The immediate causes are jealousy and secrets, but the secret exists because of the first decision, which was made under the pressure of poverty. In that way the ending follows from the opening scene, and Russell makes class part of the play's chain of cause and effect.

In conclusion, Russell shows class as a severe influence: it separates the brothers' homes, schools, work and language, it shapes the first choice that begins the plot, and it leads to deaths that the play's opening has already announced. He does not make class the only explanation, as the Narrator's question allows superstition, but he gives class a central place in the audience's final judgement.
