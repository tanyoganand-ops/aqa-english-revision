## Model answer

Lawrence and Swift both show loss as something that people deal with badly. Elizabeth Bates faces her husband's death by learning a truth about their marriage that comes too late. The boy in 'Chemistry' deals with loss by trying to act, and the result is another death that he cannot explain.

Lawrence begins by making Elizabeth's life before the loss look cold and angry. She is "a tall woman of imperious mien", and her "mouth was closed with disillusionment". Both phrases describe a woman who is proud and shut in, which is how she meets her husband's lateness. Chrysanthemums run through the story as a symbol. She tells her children, "It was chrysanthemums when I married him, and chrysanthemums when you were born", and the flowers link her marriage, her children and her husband's drunkenness. When Walter is brought home, the parlour has "a cold, deathly smell of chrysanthemums". The same flower now stands for death, so the story suggests that the marriage and the loss cannot be separated. Darkness and suffocation also build the tension. The room fills with shadow, the boy says "I canna see", and a miner reports that Walter was "smothered", so the story turns from the dark kitchen to the death underground.

The most important part of Elizabeth's grief is how she behaves, not what she says. She is practical first. She keeps the children asleep, clears the parlour floor and spreads cloths for the body. She tells her daughter that her father is asleep, with the broken words "He — he's asleep", and the dashes show how hard it is to lie. The mother-in-law grieves out loud, but Elizabeth cannot. When she sees the body she "felt countermanded", which means she has been given an order by death that she cannot obey, and the body is "impregnable". Then she sees what she did not see in life: "I have been fighting a husband who did not exist." She decides that "She had refused him as himself." This is her loss, and it is a loss of understanding as much as of a person. The story ends without comfort. She "submitted to life, which was her immediate master", but she "winced with fear and shame" from death, "her ultimate master". The repeated word "master" puts both life and death above her, so she is left powerless.

Swift presents loss through a child who tells the story as an adult looking back. The boy has already lost a grandmother and a father who disappeared on a flight, and his mother weeps and clings to him. In the first scene the model boat sinks, and the grandfather tells him to accept what cannot be got back. I read the sinking as the first sign that loss in this story is something that comes back. The narrator is a child, so he sees only part of what is happening. He is afraid of Ralph and thinks that if he spoils Ralph's face his mother will stop wanting him, so he steals acid from the grandfather's shed. The word "acid" shows how a child tries to solve grief with a physical thing. Swift makes this credible because the boy's plan is simple, secret and almost reasonable to him.

The ending of 'Chemistry' deals with a loss that the boy cannot control. His grandfather is found dead, and the official verdict is suicide. The story does not say why the old man died. The narrator believes that his mother's treatment of him was part of the reason, but that is a boy's belief and it is never confirmed, and his mother never explains. Her behaviour afterwards, including the open window and the look of relief, shows a person who deals with loss by moving on. The boy throws the acid into the pond. At the end he sees his grandfather on the far side, as he used to be when the boat crossed, and the boat seems to be moving still. The image does not end the story. It says that the lost people stay with the boy, and that he cannot let them go. This links to the grandfather's lesson that things change but are not destroyed, a point the story repeats.

Both writers are interested in what the living do not say. Elizabeth tells her children a gentle lie and says nothing to the mother-in-law about her real feelings, and the boy's mother never explains. In each story the child or children are protected by silence, and the cost is that the adult is alone. The two stories differ in their endings. Lawrence closes on a woman who has understood something, however painful. Swift closes on a boy who has not been allowed to understand.

Context supports one reading of Lawrence's story. The University of Nottingham's pages on the story say that it is set in Eastwood and based on Lawrence's experience of working-class life around Brinsley Colliery. They also say that James Lawrence, the writer's uncle and the basis for Walter Bates, was killed by a fall of coal at Brinsley Colliery in 1880. I use this as background and I do not claim that every detail in the story is factual. It suggests that the dependence of a family on a miner's wage, and the fear of sudden death, were real conditions when Lawrence wrote. This helps explain why Elizabeth counts what she would have on "the little pension" before she knows whether he is dead.

In conclusion, both stories show loss as something that people meet with fear and silence. Elizabeth grieves by seeing her husband for the first time, and the boy in 'Chemistry' grieves by acting and being left to carry the result. Lawrence ends in recognition and Swift in a haunted memory.

## Notes (my reading, not an examiner result)

- AO1: one argument (loss is met with silence and a late or partial understanding), supported by Elizabeth's changing feelings, the parlour scene and the ending, and by the child's actions in 'Chemistry'.
- AO2: the symbolism of chrysanthemums, darkness and suffocation, the dashes, the repeated "master", and the child narrator and the ending in 'Chemistry' are analysed as methods.
- AO3: the context is from two University of Nottingham pages: "Odour of Chrysanthemums" (https://www.nottingham.ac.uk/manuscriptsandspecialcollections/learning/odour.aspx) and "About the text" (http://odour.nottingham.ac.uk/about.asp), both read as fetched on 26 Nov 2026. The "pension" point is my reading of the text, not a claim from the pages. I give no context for 'Chemistry'. No single context or topic is required.
- AO4: this model aims for accurate spelling, punctuation and grammar and a varied vocabulary. I have not scored AO4.
- Limits: about 960 words, longer than a typical timed answer. The story does not settle why the grandfather died, so the answer gives only the verdict and the narrator's belief.
