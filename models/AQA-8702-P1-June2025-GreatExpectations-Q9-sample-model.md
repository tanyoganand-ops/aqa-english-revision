# Question 9 (30 marks): Great Expectations

**Question:** see the official paper for the full wording (linked above).

## Model answer

Dickens presents Miss Havisham as a victim, but not only as one. She is wronged by a man who cheats her, and then she turns that wrong into a wrong against two children. The extract shows the point where she sees this herself, and where Pip, who has been hurt by her, still pities her.

In the extract, Dickens reverses the power between them. She "dropped on her knees at my feet", and Pip says it was to his "amazement" and "terror". This is the woman who first met him seated at a table and told him to "Come nearer". Now she is on the ground, and then "down upon the ground". The picture of her hands "raised to Heaven from her mother's side" when "her poor heart was young and fresh and whole" asks for pity. It shows a girl who once prayed, before the harm was done to her.

Her cry, "What have I done! What have I done!", is repeated until it loses all meaning. Dickens lets Pip describe it with a grim exaggeration: "twenty, fifty times over, What had she done!" The repetition shows a mind stuck in a loop, like the stopped clocks of the house. Pip's judgment is careful. He knows she did "a grievous thing in taking an impressionable child to mould into the form that her wild resentment, spurned affection, and wounded pride found vengeance in". The three nouns work as a list of causes, and "spurned" and "wounded" show that her own hurt comes first. Yet the word "vengeance" and the verb "mould" keep the blame on her.

The narrator also uses images of light and disease. By "shutting out the light of day" she has "secluded herself from a thousand natural and healing influences", and her mind has "grown diseased". These are the terms of illness, and they invite sympathy as well as judgment. Pip sees "her punishment in the ruin she was", so she is both guilty and punished. Pip asks whether he could "look upon her without compassion", and the question answers itself.

The earlier chapters show how she came to this. When Pip first sees her, "the bride within the bridal dress had withered like the dress, and like the flowers", and her watch "had stopped at twenty minutes to nine". Pip's friend Herbert later tells him her story: she "passionately loved him", the man "got great sums of money from her", and the letter that ended the marriage came "At the hour and minute" at which "she afterwards stopped all the clocks". She was robbed and jilted on her wedding day, and Dickens makes the clocks the sign of a woman who cannot move on. When she says her heart is "Broken!" she says it with "a kind of boast in it", which suggests that her suffering has become her identity.

Dickens does not let this excuse what she does next. Pip hears her murmur to Estella what sounds like "Break their hearts my pride and hope, break their hearts and have no mercy!" Her own words in Chapter 49 are worse: "I stole her heart away, and put ice in its place." The image of ice shows that she knows what she took. Pip's reply is blunt, "Better," he says, "to have left her a natural heart, even to be bruised or broken", which says that being hurt would have been better for Estella than being cold. Pip himself has been a toy for her revenge, a boy there "to play". Her money ties love to power. The man "got great sums of money from her" and persuaded her to buy out her brother "on the plea that when he was her husband he must hold and manage it all". That plea fits the law described by Jill Rappoport: under the common-law doctrine of coverture a woman's property generally passed to her husband on marriage, though some women were protected by separate estates set up in trust in courts of equity, and the Married Women's Property Acts of 1870 and 1882 were later meant to give married women the status of a single woman. So Dickens ties her wounding to the risk to a woman's wealth in marriage. It also shows what her single status gave her: control of her wealth and house, and with it power over the lives of two children. A victim who makes new victims is hard to see only as a victim.

The ending gives her a chance at redemption. She asks Pip: "If you can ever write under my name, 'I forgive her,' though ever so long after my broken heart is dust pray do it!" and he does not make her wait: "I can do it now." His answer shows his own change, since he is offering a kindness he could have refused. Her repentance is real, but Dickens does not say it undoes the harm to Estella. Pip tells her that if she can "undo any scrap of what you have done amiss", it would be better "than to bemoan the past through a hundred years". He asks for action, not tears.

In conclusion, Dickens presents Miss Havisham as a victim of a man who used her love and of her own resentment, and he makes the reader pity her. He also shows that she used the power of her wealth and her house to harm others, so I would say she is a victim to a large extent but not wholly.

## Notes (my reading)

- AO1: one argument (a real victim who becomes a harmful one) tied to the extract and to Chapters 8, 12, 22 and 49 (Pip's first visit, Estella's training, Herbert's story, and the return).
- AO2: the reversal of positions, the repeated cry, the list of causes in Pip's judgment, images of light and disease, the clocks and the ice image are analysed as methods.
- AO3: the context is Rappoport's account of coverture and the later Married Women's Property Acts (BRANCH), used, with its exception for equity trusts, to explain why the plea in Herbert's story ("hold and manage it all") was plausible and why her money is both her weakness and her power. It is not a claim about every Victorian woman. No single context or topic is required.
- Section B has no AO4 mark.
- Limits: the Rappoport points were read on the BRANCH page as fetched on 26 Nov 2026. The answer is about 890 words, longer than a typical timed answer. Quotations beyond the extract were checked against the Standard Ebooks text only. Chapter numbers (8, 12, 22, 49) were read from that text's chapter headings. The answer does not discuss Estella's own account of herself, which is a choice of focus.
