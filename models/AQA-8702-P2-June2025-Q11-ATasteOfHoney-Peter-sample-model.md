## Model answer

To a large extent Delaney presents Peter as selfish and unpleasant, but she makes him more than a villain. He is wealthy, confident and sometimes fun, which makes his bad behaviour easier to believe. I will argue that Peter is shown as charming when he wants to win and cruel when he thinks he is being challenged, and that the cruelty is what the play remembers him for.

Early in the play Peter seems to have some good qualities. He has searched all over Salford to find Helen, so he wants her enough to make an effort. He brings chocolates for Jo and flowers for Helen, and he shows Jo pictures of the large house he can afford. According to the National Theatre's character notes (commentary, not dialogue), Peter is ten years younger than Helen and a wealthy car salesman who smokes Cuban cigars and takes Helen out to dinner and on weekends away. I infer that he offers her a life she has not had before. When Jo asks Peter whether he fancies Jo herself, he answers that he does not "go in for sweet young things" (a line of dialogue), which makes him seem safe to the audience. When she asks about his eye patch, he explains that he lost the eye as a private in the army. These details make him a believable man and not a caricature.

Even in these scenes, though, he is quick to look down on people. When he first sees Helen's new home, he is disgusted: "Tenements, cemetery, slaughterhouse... Nobody could live in a place like this". The list of three nouns turns the area into something to be ashamed of, and the final sentence is a judgement. Jo's reply that fifty thousand people already do live there shows what he cannot see. Peter's idea of success depends on class, and he does not hide it. The National Theatre's character notes (again commentary, not dialogue) say that he has very clear ideas about class and social hierarchy, which in the later part of the play reveal him to be an aggressive and ill-mannered man. I treat that as one reading, but this early scene supports it.

The unpleasant side becomes clear later in the play, when Peter arrives drunk. He has been waiting outside for Helen, and he takes delight in needling Jo about her pregnancy: "Who's got a bun in the oven? Who's got a cake in the stove?" The playground-style questions sound like a children's taunt, which makes the cruelty worse, because he is mocking a young woman who is alone and frightened. He then insults Geoffrey with "Who's the lily?" Calling a gay man a "lily" in the late 1950s, when the Royal Exchange pack notes that people could still be imprisoned for being gay, is a way of using society's prejudice to attack someone who is helping Jo. Peter chooses the people with least power to pick on, and Delaney lets the audience see this.

His selfishness is shown through money and control. Helen has tried to give Jo some money, and when Peter notices it he accuses her of "giving away" his money and takes it back. The act is small, but it shows that he treats Helen as someone who must account to him, and it takes support away from a pregnant girl. Peter then tells them: "I'm not having that bloody slut at our place". The word "slut" attacks Jo for being pregnant and unmarried. It also comes at the moment when Helen has just invited Jo to live with her, and Helen then leaves with him. The sequence suggests that Peter's wealth and temper let him decide how far Helen can care for her daughter, and he uses that power for himself, although the play leaves Helen's own choice open.

It is important, however, to see how far Delaney allows the audience to judge Peter alone. Helen chooses to marry him and to leave with him, and she has a history of leaving Jo, so some of the blame for Jo's isolation belongs to her. The National Theatre's synopsis also shows that Helen finally admits that Peter has left her, so his hold on her does not last. The National Theatre's character notes describe him as "confident, fun and smooth" at his best and an "intimidating drunk" at his worst. Those two phrases are the notes' commentary, not lines from the play. They suggest that his behaviour changes with drink and challenge. His behaviour is selfish and unpleasant, but the play presents him as part of a family that is already unstable.

Context supports the view that Peter's attitudes belong to his time. The Royal Exchange pack (commentary) explains that the play was written in 1958, a time of great social change, with hostility towards Black families and oppression of gay people. The National Theatre's notes (commentary) add that single mothers were treated as second-class citizens and often shunned by family and community. Peter's contempt for the pregnant, unmarried Jo, and his jokes about Geoffrey, reflect the prejudices described there. I use this as context from theatre resources and not as proof of Delaney's intention. It helps to explain why the play shows him as a man who is rewarded for his money and not challenged for his cruelty.

In conclusion, Delaney presents Peter as selfish and unpleasant to a large extent. His charm is real, and his wealth gives him power, but the scenes in which he mocks Jo, insults Geoffrey, takes back the money and refuses to have Jo near his home show his worst qualities most clearly. The play does not let Peter carry all the blame for the family's problems, but it does make him the most openly cruel of its characters.
