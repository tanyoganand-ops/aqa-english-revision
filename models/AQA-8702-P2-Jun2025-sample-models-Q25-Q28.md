# Question 2 (30 marks + AO4 4 marks): An Inspector Calls

**Question:** see the official paper for the full wording (linked above).

**Why this question:** the June 2025 paper's two Inspector Calls options are Q1 (Gerald and Sheila's changing relationship) and Q2 (power). Q2 is the sample here.

**Quotation source and limits:** the play is in copyright. Every reference below was checked against one full-text copy of a licensed US acting edition (Dramatists Play Service) found online, used for checking only. That copy is a scanned text with OCR errors and is not the UK Heinemann edition, so spelling and stage-direction wording may differ from the edition a student uses. Play references are to the act, with no quotation beyond single words.

### Model answer

In An Inspector Calls Priestley presents power as something people claim through money, class and public position, and then shows that power failing when it meets responsibility. The Birlings begin as the people who give orders. The Inspector arrives with no such status and takes control of the room, and by the end the play suggests that real power belongs to those who accept that people are bound to one another.

At the start, Mr Birling uses his public standing as a sign that he must be obeyed. When the Inspector arrives he lists his past as an alderman, a Lord Mayor and a magistrate who is still on the Bench, and he stresses that he knows the local police officers well (Act I, shortly after the Inspector's entrance). The list of offices works like credentials, and it tells the Inspector who is in charge. When questioned, he offers the language of business instead of conscience: his duty, he says, is to keep labour costs down (Act I). The word "duty" is borrowed from the Inspector, who has just said that it is his duty to ask questions (Act I), but Birling uses it to defend a refusal of fair pay, so his power over workers is shown as something he treats as a rule of business. Priestley lets him protect himself with a title again later, when he calls himself a public man (Act II). The Inspector's reply reverses the claim by saying that public men carry responsibilities as well as privileges (Act II). The balance of that sentence sets the two nouns against each other, so power is no longer a right but a debt.

Mrs Birling's power is social and moral. She claims authority as the head of a charity, and her speech is full of class judgements about a whole class of girl (Act II). The repetition shuts Eva out as a type rather than a person, and her certainty shows that she believes she can decide who deserves help. The Inspector's question breaks that confidence by making her answer for her influence: he asks her directly whether it was her influence, and offers only two possible answers (Act II). She is made to admit that it was, and her confession shows power used against someone who had none.

Priestley does not treat power only as a male or upper-class quality. Sheila, too, is shown to have wielded it. The Inspector tells her that she used the power she had, as the daughter of a good customer, to have Eva dismissed (Act I). The word "customer" shows how money can turn into influence even in the hands of a young woman who has no official position, and Sheila's later agreement shows a character learning to see her power for what it did. The play gives each member of the family a different relation to power. Sheila comes to see hers, and her parents do not, which is Priestley's way of setting the younger generation's willingness to learn against the older generation's refusal.

The structure turns on the Inspector's arrival. Before he comes, Birling talks confidently about war, business and his hopes of a knighthood, and the family sits at ease, with the port and the engagement. When the Inspector enters, the conversation is controlled by his questions, and he can silence Birling with a blunt order not to ramble or whine at him (Act II). The imperative, and the single word "man" that ends it, take away the courtesy that the Birlings expect. Priestley's technique is that the Inspector has no rank in the room and only one tool, the question, and it is enough. Each revelation shifts the balance: Sheila's confession, then Gerald's, then Mrs Birling's, so that the authority of the Birlings is taken apart piece by piece.

At the end, the family tries to take power back. Birling's response to the Inspector's exit is practical and class-based: he wants to telephone the Chief Constable, a man of his own circle (Act III). He still believes that connections to men of rank can restore order, and the family ends the play dividing into those who feel the Inspector's warning and those who want to dismiss it. The play was written in 1945 and set before the First World War, so for its first audiences the Inspector's final warning, that people will be taught responsibility through suffering if they refuse to learn it (Act III), would carry the weight of what had happened since. That is a point about the play's date and setting, not a claim about every audience. The Inspector's own idea of power is not command but shared duty: people belong to one community (Act III).

In conclusion, Priestley presents power as something the Birlings assume belongs to class and office, and shows it as empty when it is used against the powerless. The Inspector's real power is moral. He can make the family answer, and the play leaves the audience to ask who will.

### Notes on the answer (my reading, not an examiner result)

- AO1: one argument (class power is exposed, moral responsibility replaces it) with the key figures covered: Birling, the Inspector's control, Gerald, Mrs Birling, Eva.
- AO2: methods linked to quotations: the list of offices, the word "duty", the balanced sentence on responsibilities and privileges, the repeated class label, the two-answer question, the blunt imperative.
- AO3: social class and power, the Inspector's alternative view, and the 1945 writing date set against the pre-First World War setting. Attitudes to the sexes are touched only through Sheila.
- AO4 (4 marks) is for accurate spelling, punctuation, grammar and vocabulary. I make no claim about marks. The model aims to be clean on those points.
- Weak spot: the Sheila paragraph gives her change in general terms with only one reference.

# Question 25 (30 marks): Follower and Before You Were Mine

**Question:** see the official paper for the full wording (linked above).

**Poem chosen for the comparison:** 'Before You Were Mine' by Carol Ann Duffy. This is a choice for the sample. Any other poem from the cluster would also be a valid choice.

### Model answer

Both poems present growing up as a change in how a child sees a parent. In 'Follower' Heaney admires his father and wants to become him, then watches the roles reverse. In 'Before You Were Mine' Duffy looks back at a mother who was young before she was a parent, and the speaker grows up by seeing her as a separate person. In both, the child's feelings are mixed with love, and in both time moves the relationship on.

Heaney begins with pride in his father's skill. The poem opens on the father's horse-plough (line 1), and the image of his shoulders swelling like a ship's sail (line 2) makes the man large and graceful, a vessel carried by the work itself. The details of the craft, the setting of the wing and the fitting of the steel point (lines 5 to 6), sound technical and sure. A two-word sentence of approval at line 5 gives the judgement the weight of a child's certainty. The father's eye, narrowed and angled at the ground, is said to map the furrow exactly (lines 11 to 12), which suggests a precision the boy could only watch. The verb "map" turns ploughing into something close to an art.

The child's growing up begins as imitation. The boy wants to grow up and plough, and to copy even the way his father closes one eye and stiffens an arm (lines 17 to 18). Yet the next two lines deflate this: all he ever did was follow in the father's broad shadow (lines 19 to 20). The word "shadow" suggests both protection and being overshadowed, and "broad" carries on the sense of the father's size. The title word 'Follower' is the poem's judgement on the child's role. The adult speaker remembers himself as a nuisance, tripping, falling and yapping (lines 21 to 22), and the present participles make the child's mess go on and on. The comment is gentle, because it is the father's patience we sense behind it.

Then the time frame turns. In the last two lines it is the father who now keeps stumbling behind the speaker (lines 23 to 24). The verb "stumbling" repeats the child's earlier falling, so the poem has a circle: the one who fell is now the one followed. The final clause, saying that the father will not go away, can be read as a grown son feeling his father's presence as a burden, or as a refusal to let go of someone he loves, and Heaney leaves both in view. The speaker's feelings about growing up are admiration, then the sadness that growing up means the parent grows old.

Duffy's poem begins from the other end. Her speaker is not yet born (line 6). The adult child imagines the corner where the mother laughs with her friends (lines 1 to 2), and places herself ten years away from that moment. The named friends make the mother real and separate. Where Heaney's father is defined by work, Duffy's mother is defined by freedom: her dress blows round her legs (line 5). The one-word sentence "Marilyn" at the end of that stanza connects her to a film star and to glamour, and the mother is allowed to be the star of her own story.

The speaker's growing up is therefore an act of imagination. Duffy shows the child's later place in that story as a shift from joy to claim, when the speaker thinks of the time before her own loud, possessive yell (line 11). The adjectives present the child as an interruption, much as Heaney's "nuisance" does, but here the self-criticism is wry, ending in a question to the mother. Both poets look back at themselves as a burden and do so with affection. The memory of dancing at line 16 is a more direct picture of what the mother gave the child, and the image of stamping stars in the next line turns the ordinary street into something bright.

The poems differ in how they end. Heaney finishes on a reversal that is present and unresolved: his father is still there, behind him. Duffy ends in a wish that holds the mother in the past, a glamorous love that lasts where she dances before she was the speaker's mother (lines 19 to 20). The possessive "mine" returns in the last line, so that the child's claim on the mother is both the cause of the poem's love and the thing that closes her youth. Growing up, for Duffy, includes recognising the parent's life that the child was not part of. For Heaney it means taking the parent's place and finding that the place carries responsibility.

In terms of the speaker's relation to the parent, both poems are from an adult looking back, so the child's feelings are filtered by adult understanding. Heaney writes in a quiet, controlled form, regular four-line stanzas with rhymes such as "sod" and "plod", which suits an orderly, skilled father. Duffy's long, loose lines run over their ends, and the images of the ballroom and the movie future (lines 6 to 7) echo the mother's excitement rather than a farm's discipline. The forms match what each speaker admires.

Each poem also gives the parent a particular world. Heaney's boy learns by watching a skilled man at work on the land. Duffy's mother has a girlhood of street and "Mass", where a hiding for coming home late is part of her ordinary evening (lines 9 to 10). These details make each parent specific, and they suggest that in these two poems growing up involves stepping out of that world while still loving it.

In conclusion, both poets present growing up as a loving but unequal relationship in which the child slowly sees the parent more fully. Heaney's feeling is admiration turning to protective sadness. Duffy's is delight in a mother's youth with a hint of loss. The comparison shows that growing up is not only leaving a parent behind, but also understanding who they were.

### Notes on the answer (my reading, not an examiner result)

- Structure: the first two sections treat 'Follower' and 'Before You Were Mine' in turn, then the later sections compare them directly (the speakers' looking-back perspective, the ending, form). It is a block structure with a sustained comparison from the fifth section on, not a comparison in every paragraph. Quotations are short and exact.
- AO2: methods named where they support a point: the two-word sentence at Follower line 5, "shadow", the repeated "stumbling", the single-word sentence "Marilyn", the named friends, the possessive "mine", regular stanzas with rhyme against long running lines.
- AO3: context is limited to what the poems show: farm work, and the world of her street and "Mass". The answer does not claim facts about either poet's life that cannot be checked from the printed texts.
- Length: about 900 words. This is a teaching model, not a timed target.

# Question 28.1 (24 marks): 'Table'

**Question:** see the official paper for the full wording (linked above).

### Model answer

Hull presents home and family as something so full of shared life that it cannot be changed or cleared away. The table stands for the family, and the speakers' feelings about it are mixed: it is awkward, but they love it.

The poem opens with a plan to sell the table (section 1 of the archive text). The phrase of intention announces an intention that is then dropped. The table is described as big where it stands, with edges that seem to elbow the household and corners that push people into corners, so it becomes a physical pressure. The repeated word "corners" plays on its two meanings, the table's corners and a person being cornered, so the first section already suggests that family life can feel crowded and hard to escape.

Then the speakers change course (section 2). They had planned to rub down the surface. The things to be removed are damage, yet the phrase for the stains is almost gentle, because a stain is likened to a moon. The poet has made the marks beautiful. The burns, dents and stains are permanent, and they suggest that family life leaves lasting traces both good and bad. Even the stuck scrap of newsprint is a small record of a particular day. In the next section they say they have not even started cleaning the old table (section 3).

The reason is given in the long list that follows (sections 4 and 5), built on the repeated "too". The first list is of everyday family activities done on the table, from caring for babies to children's noise and drawing. The word "too" normally complains, but here it works as the opposite: there is so much life that the speakers cannot bring themselves to undo it. The list follows a family growing up. It then adds darker items: homework and grief are set next to laughter and candles burned low. "Grief" and "laughter" are placed together, and so the table holds the whole range of family life. The verb "dumped" is blunt, suggesting how trouble was simply put down on the nearest surface, and the burned-down candles suggest celebration and the passing of time.

The plural "we" runs through the poem. No single person is named; the decisions and feelings are shared, so the home is presented as a joint life. It also makes the speakers' reluctance to act feel like agreement rather than argument.

The ending returns to the table's awkward shape (sections 5 and 6): it must stay where it is, in an awkward place. The last word is the poem's point. The table acts like a person, "elbowing" and "reminding", and its edges, first a nuisance, are now a means of memory. The first section planned to sell it; the last accepts it. The poem's structure moves from pressure to acceptance, and "awkward" is kept to the end, so the poet does not pretend that home is simple.

In conclusion, Hull presents home and family as a mixture of joy and grief whose marks cannot and should not be removed, and the feeling that results is a rueful, loving acceptance.

### Notes on the answer (my reading, not an examiner result)

- Covers memories, a mixture of good and bad, and permanence in a developing argument about acceptance.
- AO2 points are tied to quotations: "corners", the moon-like stain, the burn and dent, the repeated "too" pattern, "grief" next to "laughter", the plural "we", first and last lines.
- Not covered: tone beyond "rueful", line-length or stanza form, which the task does not require.

# Question 28.2 (8 marks): 'Like a Beacon' and 'Table'

**Question:** see the official paper for the full wording (linked above).

### Model answer

Both poems make a single everyday thing carry feelings about home, but they use different voices and different tones. In 'Like a Beacon' the speaker craves her mother's food (lines 3 to 4), and the repeated statement of need (lines 8 to 9) is direct and personal. The singular "I" and the short, plain lines make the feeling clear and urgent. In 'Table' the pronoun is the plural "we", so the feeling belongs to a family, and the table is described through a long list of marks and damage, and the repeated "too" (sections 4 and 5) shows years of shared life.

Food and furniture are both symbols of home, but they work differently. Nichols's plantains and saltfish (lines 6 to 7) are specific, and food is a way of staying linked to her mother while she is far away, in London, and carrying it against the cold (line 13). The simile in the title, repeated at line 12, turns the bag of food into a signal light, so the feeling is hope and belonging. Hull's table is not something the speakers lack but something they cannot be rid of, as its edges keep elbowing them (section 6), so his feeling is mixed: the marks of burn and dent sit alongside laughter. Nichols's tone is unambiguous longing, Hull's is more complicated and accepting.

The titles also differ. 'Beacon' looks outward, a light that guides across distance. 'Table' is a fixed object that stays where it is, in its awkward place, as home does.

### Notes on the answer (my reading, not an examiner result)

- AO2 only: no context or theme-only marks are being earned, so the answer stays on methods (voice, simile, symbol, list, title, tone). It makes comparison in every paragraph.
- Line numbers are the printed lines of the poems in the question paper. 'Table' is cited by section of the Children's Poetry Archive page, because it is not printed in the paper.
- About 270 words. This is a teaching model, not a timed target.
