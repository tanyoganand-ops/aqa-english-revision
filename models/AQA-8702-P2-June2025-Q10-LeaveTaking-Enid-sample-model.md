## Model answer

Pinnock presents Enid as strong in what she does for her family, but not always strong in how she treats the people in it. She works, plans, protects and acts at once in a crisis. She also controls, dismisses and hides her feelings, and by the end she is tired and in need of care herself. I think the play asks its audience to admire her strength and to see what it costs her.

Enid's strength is clearest in her hopes for her daughters. She has persuaded herself that her sacrifices were worth it and that the girls' future is in England. She believes that hard work, keeping to the rules and perseverance will bring acceptance, and in the second scene she says England has been good to her and that she is proud of her "English" daughters. That belief is the engine of her motherhood. She wants Viv and Del to succeed, and the final scene gives her some proof: she tells Del that Viv is getting ready to go to university to read Black Studies. A mother who has pushed her child that far has achieved something real, and Pinnock lets the audience see it.

Her strength also shows in the way she deals with trouble. She does not trust England's rules alone. In the first scene she takes her doubts about the constant requests for money to Mai, who is described by AQA as an Obeah woman, and so she looks for guidance within her own culture. When the telephone call in the third scene brings news that her mother has died, Pinnock's stage direction reads: "As ENID listens something drains out of her. She drops the phone" (p. 40). The direction shows the shock in her body, and what follows shows her character: without question she makes immediate plans to send money. In the worst moment the play shows a woman who acts. It also settles the earlier doubt, because the death shows that Mai's advice about the money was right.

However, Pinnock also shows the limits of that strength. Enid sees Viv mainly through her own ambitions. When Viv wants to visit Jamaica, Enid blames Brod: "You see what nonsense you put in the girl head?" (p. 39). The word "nonsense" is dismissive, and it shows that she does not understand that Viv, too, is searching for roots and identity. Brod's view of the girls is different. He tells Enid that they have "Caribbean souls" (p. 29) and that "Those girls ain't wild, Enid" (p. 36). Because Brod is a friend from Enid's own childhood in Jamaica, his criticism is hard for her to dismiss, and the audience sees her judged by someone who knows her. The second scene also builds to an argument between Enid and Del that ends in a slap. I take this as a moment of severity, although I do not claim more than that, since I do not know from my sources who strikes whom.

The third scene shows another side of Enid. She hums a hymn while she tidies, and when the pastor's visit is over she asks Brod: "It look bad that Viv and Del wasn't here?" (p. 35). The question shows that she craves the pastor's approval and wants her family to look respectable. That is a kind of weakness, because a strong mother would not need that confirmation, but it is also very human. Pinnock sets this beside Brod's different view of religion, which he sees as a source of unity "back home" and as an obsession with respectability in England, so Enid's anxiety is placed in a wider picture.

The ending changes the question. At the end of the fourth scene Enid says: "I want... I want to go home" (p. 45). In the last scene Del challenges her about whether she loves her, and Enid speaks about feeling disliked by her own mother. She says she knows how Del is treated, that she would do anything for her children, that she has fought to get them this far, and that she is tired. This is the start of repairing their relationship, and it shows that Enid, who has always given, now needs someone to look after her. The play ends with Del taking her mother's hand and smoothing it for a palm reading. The gesture reverses the earlier pattern, since the mother who always gave is now being given something. I read this as the play's answer to the question: Enid is strong because she has carried so much, but her honesty at the end makes the strength more human.

Context supports this reading. Enid, Brod and Mai probably came to England after the Second World War, and Brod describes how he once thought of himself as a British subject who waved a flag on Empire Day until a letter told him to put his nationality papers in order or be sent away. The Nick Hern guide links this to immigration law and says Brod does not feel like a secure British citizen. I use it as background from one secondary source, not as proof of what Pinnock meant. It suggests that Enid's drive for respectability and success for her daughters could be a response to insecurity, so her strictness is not simply a fault.

In conclusion, Pinnock presents Enid as a strong mother to a large extent. She works, plans, protects and acts in a crisis. But the play also shows her as dismissive of Viv, hard on Del and reluctant to speak about her past, and the ending shows that her strength has left her tired. Pinnock presents her as strong and flawed, and the flaws make her more believable.

## Notes (my reading, not an examiner result)

- AO1: one argument (strong in what she does, limited in how she listens, honest at the end) tied to named moments in the first, second, third, fourth and final scenes.
- AO2: Brod's view of Enid as a way of judging her, the stage direction on p. 40, the slap, the final palm-reading gesture, and the hymn and domestic setting.
- AO3: context (post-war migration, immigration law, belonging, period tension) comes from the Nick Hern guide and is hedged as one source. Parents, heritage and generational difference are woven into the argument. No single context is required.
- AO4: aimed at accurate spelling, punctuation and varied vocabulary. Not scored.
- Source limits: quotations come only from the guide's printing of the play and could not be checked against the playscript. The slap is stated without who strikes whom because the sources do not say.
