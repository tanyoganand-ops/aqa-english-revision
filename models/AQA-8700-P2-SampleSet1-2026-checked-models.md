# Question 2 (8 marks): infer the differences between the trains

**Question:** see the official paper for the full wording (linked above).

### Model answer

The two trains belong to different stages of railway history, and the writers' descriptions let us infer how far railways have changed between the 1830 ride and the 1933 journey.

Source A describes a long, complete express. It carries a mail van and a dining-car at the front (line 3) and five sleeping cars behind (line 19), with an engine of its own, so it serves several purposes at once: it carries post, feeds people and lets them sleep. A journey of more than a week over nine thousand kilometres, which the introduction tells us about, can only be made by a train designed to look after its passengers for days. The writer's complaints, such as the shortage of butter (line 37) and the dust that stops windows opening, show how the train provides comforts to the point that a lack of them annoys him, which suggests that passengers now expect a train to be more than transport.

Source B describes a very early railway. The carriage is "a long-bodied vehicle with seats placed across it, back-to-back", and "a sort of uncovered carriage", so there is no shelter. It has "six of these benches" and carries "a party of sixteen persons", a small number compared with the dining-car and sleeping cars of Source A. The engine is "the little train engine", made of "a boiler, a stove, a small platform, a bench, and behind the bench a barrel", so it is simple enough to list in one sentence, and the writer sits on its bench with the engineer. We can infer that the train is an experiment: its purpose is to show what the machine can do ("thirty-five miles an hour, swifter than a bird flies"), not to supply comfort.

The difference is therefore one of development and purpose. Source A's train is large, specialised and built for long-distance, comfortable travel; Source B's is small, basic and built to demonstrate a new invention. Source B's writer is amazed by a journey of "fifteen miles", while Source A's writer has spent over a week on his, and that contrast in distance and in the facilities needed for it points to how much the railway had grown.

### Notes (my reading, not an examiner result)

- Aim: inferences from both texts (stage of development, purpose, facilities, size) with well-chosen references and explicit differences. It deals with both texts and the focus, the difference between the trains.
- The words inference and "differences" in the stem are in the sample paper. A response to the 2025 stem (summary-style) would use the same material.

# Question 3 (12 marks): language of the train crash

**Question:** see the official paper for the full wording (linked above).

### Model answer

Fleming describes the crash in two ways. At first it is violent and personal. Then it becomes a comic scene, and the change in language shows how he decides to treat the whole event.

The crash begins with sound and impact: a frightful jarring followed by a crash (line 12). "Frightful" is a strong adjective and "crash" imitates the noise, so the reader hears the moment as well as seeing it. The writer then makes the violence personal: his suitcase is "cannonaded" down onto his knees (line 13). The verb "cannonaded" belongs to warfare, so the suitcase becomes a weapon fired at him, and the alliteration in the phrase about fearful force (line 13) gives the line weight. His reaction is exaggerated for humour as well as shock: he thinks it is the end of the world (line 14). The hyperbole is undercut by the casual extra complaint about his legs that follows (line 14), so Fleming is already laughing at his panic. The carriage becomes his "world" (line 15), and it is tilted, with the adverb "drunkenly" giving it a comic, unsteady movement.

After that the language shifts. A run of short sentences ends with Fleming feeling very annoyed (line 16). The tone is flat and ordinary, so the writer treats the crash as an inconvenience, and "annoyed" is far smaller than the end of the world (line 14). Once outside, a description of a spectacular scene (line 17) makes the setting pleasant, and the adverb "refreshingly" suggests that the crash has done him good.

The train itself is personified. It sprawls down the embankment (line 18), like a person who has fallen, and the cars lie in attitudes that grow less grotesque the further back they are (line 20). The formal verb "disposed" and the adjective "grotesque" are set against "primly", which suggests a well-behaved passenger who has refused to join the chaos. The engine, dug in and snorting steam (line 22), is treated like an animal, and its naughty look and its consciousness of indiscretion (line 23) make it a child that knows it has misbehaved. Fleming describes a disaster in the language of manners, so that the reader sees the crash as he does, as a comedy.

### Notes (my reading, not an examiner result)

- Stays within lines 12 to 23. Uses terminology accurately (onomatopoeia, alliteration, hyperbole, personification, adverbs, short sentences, juxtaposition) and explains the effect each time.
- Range and analysis of effect matter more than naming many terms.

# Question 4 (16 marks): compare the writers' feelings and perspectives

**Question:** see the official paper for the full wording (linked above).

### Model answer

The two writers write about trains in opposite moods. Fleming is a tired passenger at the end of a long journey who treats even a crash as entertainment. Kemble is a young woman on her first ride who is overcome by wonder. They also use different methods to put those feelings across.

Fleming begins with relief. The end-of-term feeling (line 2) is about escape, and the repeated negative opening of his list in the first paragraph (line 3) turns his journey into a list of things he is glad to be leaving. The pattern of the repetition imitates the monotony he describes. His food is "rancid" (line 4), a word that makes the bread repulsive. Even when the train crashes, he treats it as a treat, a comic and violent climax (line 27). His perspective is that of someone who has seen too much of the same days and welcomes any change. He turns the train into an enemy, a prison (line 37). The personification gives the train a will to bully, and the short, flat sentences at the end (line 37) sound final.

Kemble starts from joy. "A normal sheet of writing paper is enough for love, but only a large sheet can contain my raptures" sets her feelings above even love, and "raptures" suggests something close to a religious thrill. Where Fleming is world-weary, she is open to novelty, and where he sees the train as a prison, she sees an animal she "felt rather inclined to pat". She uses figurative language in the opposite direction: the engine is a "steam-horse" with "oats" and "reins", and "this brave little she-dragon", so the new machine is made friendly and magical. Her sentences also differ. Fleming's are dry and sardonic; hers run on with exclamatory, admiring phrases, "lovely and wonderful beyond all words" and "no fairy tale was ever half so wonderful as what I saw". Her perspective is that of a first-time traveller who has "not the slightest fear", while Fleming has had so much of the train that he is not afraid even when it crashes.

Both writers personify their trains, but for different purposes. Fleming's engine has a naughty look (line 23), which is mocking and affectionate, and he writes about it as a defeated opponent, a broken toy (line 38). Kemble's engine is a creature to love. Both also use humour, but Fleming's is irony and Kemble's is delight.

So the contrast is between a writer who has used up the novelty of travel and a writer who is just discovering it. Each of them uses personification and figurative language, but Fleming uses it to belittle the train and Kemble to admire it.

### Notes (my reading, not an examiner result)

- Aim: a sustained comparison of feelings and perspectives, with methods analysed in both texts (repetition and listing, metaphor, personification, sentence length, exclamation). It uses detail from both throughout and covers whole texts.
- The feelings-and-perspectives focus is the organising idea.

# Question 5 (40 marks): speech

**Question:** see the official paper for the full wording (linked above).

### Model piece (about 430 words, arguing for the statement)

Councillors, thank you for letting me speak.

Let me begin by agreeing with half of the statement. Cars are convenient. They are comfortable. For many families in this town, with two jobs, a school run and a supermarket twenty minutes away, they are the only way the day works. I am not here to take anyone's keys.

I am here to ask why so many of us feel we have no choice.

Last month I tried to get to the hospital appointment I made in the spring, by bus. There is one bus an hour, and it stops running at six. The fare for a single journey cost more than my sandwich and my coffee together. I arrived late, and I was one of the lucky ones, because I could walk. Ask anyone who cannot, and they will tell you what accessibility means in practice: a step that is too high, a timetable that is out of date and a stop with no shelter.

When public transport is expensive, unreliable and hard to use, people who can afford a car buy one, and people who cannot are left behind. That is not a choice. That is a gap.

And the gap costs us all. Every morning, the road past the high school is a queue of engines running. Parents sit in their cars, worried about the children walking beside them, and breathing the fumes that the children also breathe. If ten of those families had a bus that came every ten minutes and cost less than petrol, how many cars would still be there?

Some of you will say this is expensive. I would ask you to count the cost of doing nothing: the potholes made by heavy traffic, the hours lost in jams, the shops in our town centre where nobody can park, and the young people who cannot take a job because they cannot reach it.

So here is what I am asking. Cap the fare for young people and for those over sixty. Guarantee that the last bus runs later in the evening. Put a shelter, a clear timetable and step-free boarding at every stop. These are not dreams. They are what people in other towns already have.

Cars will always have their place. But a town in which the only reliable way to move is to own an engine is a town that has made a decision for its poorest residents without asking them.

Make public transport something worth choosing. Do that, and I promise you the roads will be quieter, the air will be cleaner, and the people in this room will find that the car was never the only way home.

Thank you.

### Notes (my reading, not an examiner result)

- AO5: speech register (direct address, a personal example, a concession, a call to action, rhetorical questions) with a clear structure and a controlled close. AO6: varied sentences, accurate punctuation. I make no claim about marks.
- Original text, written for this model. It argues one side; the statement allows either.
- Sample Set 1 only: the mark scheme has a note under the AO5 grid (page 20, https://www.aqa.org.uk/files/14a75f30-f517-4371-befe-969d2ec0f800/f55d9f498261132baf0bcbd55fd4b2cedcd90a5c.pdf) saying that a response that does not directly address the focus of the task cannot be clear or convincing, so AO5 is capped at the top of Level 2 (12 of 24 marks). The note does not define "focus" further. It does not apply to AO6. Read the task wording first and answer that task.
