# Question 2 (8 marks, AO1 synthesis), both sources

**Question:** see the official paper for the full wording (linked above).

### Model answer

In Source A the peregrine falcon lives in a human-made habitat, the city. It sits on the roof of Tate Modern, and Moss explains that humans have put up tall buildings which serve just as well as the high cliffs peregrines need for nests and for watching their territory (lines 35 to 36). The city also gives the falcons food, a plentiful supply of birds to hunt (lines 37 to 38), and safety, because people now protect them. This habitat has changed, since peregrines used to be birds of remote, wild places (line 27), and the birds have adapted to it.

In Source B the birds live in a natural, rural habitat around a brook. They nest in many different places, hidden from view: blackbirds and thrushes in the hawthorns, the nettle-creeper "among the stalks" in the knotted grasses, wood-pigeons in the ash trees and willows, and starlings in a hollow in the oak. The brook is a shared resource, because the birds "built by it" and "came to it to drink".

The main difference is that the falcon uses height, open space and human buildings to hunt, whereas the birds in Jefferies' fields use cover, water and natural features, which suggests safe, hidden nesting and a habitat that has changed little over time.


# Question 3 (12 marks, AO2 language), Source A lines 11 to 20

**Question:** see the official paper for the full wording (linked above).

### Model answer

Moss describes the peregrine as a powerful, precise hunter, and he does it through a run of violent images. The opening simile compares the falcon to a guided missile (line 13). The comparison suggests speed and aggression, and the adjective "guided" adds control, as if the attack has a purpose and cannot miss. The verb phrase that follows continues the military idea, and the falcon's fixed gaze on its prey (line 13) turns the pigeon into an object, which makes the falcon seem cold and focused.

The verbs are active and in the present tense. He "powers" up, "rises" and then stops, turns and folds (lines 11 to 12), and later "brakes" and "extrudes" (lines 16 to 17). The present tense puts the reader inside the hunt as it happens, and the short list of actions (line 12) separates the stages of the dive so that each one feels deliberate. The statistic for his diving speed (line 14) adds a factual detail, which makes the speed seem astonishing and real.

Sound and image make the moment of the strike dramatic. The simile for the talons (line 17) compares the claws to folding knives, so the bird is presented as a hidden weapon that opens at the last moment. The second simile (line 17) compares the sound of the capture to a whip. The adjective "explosive" and the onomatopoeic "crack" make the capture sudden and loud, after the near-silence of the dive. The pigeon is unaware of what is coming (line 14), and the noun "fate" suggests that the outcome is already decided.

The end of the paragraph changes the picture. The word "plunder" suggests a pirate taking treasure, but the picture of food for his hungry chicks (line 19) shows that the predator is also a parent. This contrast makes the falcon more than a killer, and the reader is left admiring him.


# Question 4 (16 marks, AO3 comparison), both sources

**Question:** see the official paper for the full wording (linked above).

### Model answer

Both writers feel admiration and affection for the natural world, and both present humans as part of it, but they convey those feelings in different ways.

Moss is amazed and proud. He opens with a regal simile comparing the falcon to an emperor (line 1), which gives the falcon status and makes the reader admire it before it has done anything. His pride is clearest in the second half of the source, where he describes how attitudes have changed, from persecuting birds to being able to offer protection (line 40). The alliteration of "persecuting" and "protection" sets two opposite attitudes against each other, and the inclusive pronoun "we" makes the reader share the credit. The ending, with its words for resilience and adaptability (line 44), is hopeful, but the warning that Britain's wildlife is entering a critical period (line 45) adds concern, so his feelings are admiration mixed with urgency.

Jefferies also feels deep affection for nature, but his tone is nostalgic and calm. He starts with the personal statement "The brook has forgotten me, but I have not forgotten the brook", and the reversal shows a loving, one-sided memory. Memory is compared to lightning that reveals "the pictures in the mind", so the scene is vivid and lasting. His central idea comes in the short sentence "All life loved the brook." The monosyllabic words and the soft alliteration of "life loved" make it sound simple and gentle, and the word "All" includes every creature, from birds and cattle to children. Humans here are not in charge. They sit among the living things that need the water.

This is a key difference in the relationship with humans. In Source A people are managers: they have put up tall buildings (line 36) and they offer protection (line 40), so nature depends on human choices. In Source B the country girls, the children and the spaniel are simply part of the same community of life, and Jefferies even notes that the apple tree blossoms for itself: "They did not grow for human admiration".

The methods differ as well. Moss uses present-tense action and dramatic similes (such as the guided missile, line 13) and statistics (lines 25 and 28) to build excitement and evidence. Jefferies uses the past tense, long flowing sentences and imagery such as "nails of gold" for buttercups to build a gentle, golden, remembered world. Both texts therefore convey love of nature, one through energy and evidence, the other through memory and image.


# Question 5 (40 marks: 24 AO5, 16 AO6), article

**Question:** see the official paper for the full wording (linked above).

### Model answer (practice example)

**Give us a garden, not just a car park**

Walk across our site at break and count how many people are looking at the sky. Almost nobody. We stare at phones, queue for the canteen or hurry between tarmac and concrete. I want to argue that this is a mistake, and that our school should give us proper green areas where students and teachers can spend time with nature.

The first reason is health. Many of us are tired and anxious, particularly during exam season, and many people find that time among plants and animals calms the mind. Even a few minutes of watching birds or touching soil can feel like a pause button, and a short walk under trees is a better reset than another screen. A garden would not be a luxury. It would be a free, daily form of wellbeing support.

Some people will say that we already have a field and a sports hall. But exercise and nature are not the same thing. A football pitch is a place for competition, whereas a pond, a flower bed or a wildlife corner invites us to slow down and notice. Staff need this too: teachers spend their days indoors and under pressure, and a quiet green space would help them to stay patient and well.

There is also a learning benefit. Imagine a biology class watching real pollinators on real flowers, or a creative writing group sitting under a tree to describe the morning. Learning outdoors turns abstract ideas into lived experience, and students remember what they have touched and seen.

Finally, a school garden would teach responsibility. If students planted and looked after a hedge or a bed of herbs, they would see the results of their care, just as the volunteers who protect London's peregrines have helped a once-rare bird to return. Small actions, repeated every day, can change a place.

Of course, spaces cost money and take effort. Yet a bench, a few planters and a bird feeder cost far less than the new equipment we often celebrate, and volunteers from every year group could do much of the work. The question is not whether we can afford a green space, but whether we can afford to go without one.

So I urge our leaders to act. Turn a corner of the car park into a garden, put trees where there is bare concrete, and let us look up. We would be healthier, calmer and more curious, and so would our teachers.

This is a provisional self-assessment against the published descriptors. An examiner would judge each strand holistically and could place it differently.

AO5 content and organisation (24 marks). Strengths: a clear point of view argued through a series of linked reasons (health, difference from sport, learning, responsibility, cost); a counter-argument is answered; an engaging headline and a direct opening that suits a school website article; a persuasive, slightly formal but student-friendly register; and a closing call to action that returns to the opening image of looking up. This fits the Level 3 to Level 4 descriptors on register, structure and ideas. Limitations: the vocabulary is mostly clear and controlled, not especially wide-ranging, the health claims are general (the exam does not require sources, so this is a limit on development, not a penalty), and the paragraphs open with plain connectives ("The first reason", "There is also", "Finally"). So I would not claim the upper Level 4 range (22 to 24) for this answer.

AO6 technical accuracy (16 marks). Strengths: secure sentence demarcation, a mix of short and long sentences, accurate spelling, and controlled Standard English. Limitation: the vocabulary is varied but mostly plain, and the punctuation is accurate and controlled, with commas, full stops, apostrophes and a colon used correctly. I read it as the upper part of Level 3 to Level 4, judged as an overall picture of accuracy and control.

Practice example note: the article is long for study purposes. It is not a word-count target and does not show what can be written in the advised 45 minutes, which includes planning and checking.
