## Provisional study response

Syal sets Anita and Me in a small, close village, and through Tollington she shows a society in which change arrives and some people respond with fear. The Kumars are the only Indian family in the village, so every shift in the community, from a new road to a new kind of neighbour, is seen through Meena's eyes. I will argue that Tollington is presented as a community under pressure, where worry about change turns into prejudice, and that the same setting is where Meena begins to understand who she is.

The setting first makes Meena's difference visible. Meena and her parents are the only Indian family in a West Midlands village, and their culture and traditions are very different from those of her classmates. She is caught between a rural, white, working-class village and her Punjabi heritage. In a place this small everyone knows who belongs, so Meena's wish to fit in, which leads her to befriend Anita, makes sense. She feels unsure of her place in Tollington and unsure of a connection to Indian culture, and this unhappiness leads her to make bad decisions. The setting therefore shapes her behaviour as well as her identity.

Tollington is also a mining village, and the wider region has a history of decline. By the mid-twentieth century the industries of the Black Country were falling away, coal mines closed through the 1960s, and many jobs were lost in the late 1970s and early 1980s. Some people resisted changes to their communities and blamed immigrants for the shortage of work. This is background from an exam board's teacher guide and not a description of the novel, but it helps explain why a village with this past might feel threatened by change.

The motorway makes that anxiety concrete. Locals are angry about a planned motorway that will destroy parts of Tollington, and at the local fete Sam Lowbridge, who is usually kind to Meena, angrily criticises the project and shouts racist insults. Racial tension grows when work starts on the road. Diggers arrive to demolish the local primary school and an Indian man is seen overseeing the work, which adds to the tension. Sam shouts a racist slogan into a local news camera, and the next day the Indian man is attacked and robbed. The anger about a physical change, the loss of part of the village, is turned against a person. A road is a change that ordinary villagers cannot control, and in Tollington the fear it causes is directed at someone from outside the community.

Racism is also part of daily life. Meena is unexpectedly called a racist name by an elderly woman, the name of Anita's dog is a racist insult, and some people use a different name for Meena because they say hers is too difficult. Some of this racism is deliberate and some comes from ignorance, so that people do not realise how hurtful it is. Meena feels it in her body. When Sam is racist towards one of her "uncles", she says: "I felt as if I had been punched in the stomach." The simile makes the effect physical. Society's attitudes are not abstract for Meena, because she feels them as a blow.

Friendship brings the same tensions into private life. Anita bullies her friends and is impressed by Sam's racist behaviour. When Meena overhears Anita describing how she and Sam took part in a racist attack on an Indian man, she is shocked. The prejudice of the village therefore breaks into the one friendship through which Meena had hoped to belong.

Meena's response to the village changes as she learns more about her heritage. Her grandmother, Nanima, arrives from India to help when Meena's mother has a baby, and she inspires Meena to be proud of her heritage by telling her stories and teaching her Punjabi. She also helps Meena to understand that her parents came to England to give her a better life and education. By the end, Meena has learned to embrace her Indian heritage and recognises that, however hard she tries to be like the other children, a part of her will always be different. Meena's friendship with Robert in hospital, and the Kumars' friendships with other Indian families, show that belonging can be found outside the village's idea of who fits in.

The ending shows that the village has limits. Meena tells the truth when Tracey wants to say she was pushed into a pond, passes the eleven-plus exam, and her family leaves Tollington to start a new life in Wolverhampton. The move is not only an escape. Meena leaves having changed, with a firmer sense of her heritage.

Context supports this reading. The author, Meera Syal, was born in 1963 and grew up in a mining village in the West Midlands after her family moved from New Delhi in 1960, and the novel is semi-autobiographical. After the Second World War Britain encouraged immigration from Europe and the Commonwealth, and the Commonwealth Immigrants Act of 1962 later put more limits on immigrants. This background suggests that a village like Tollington could have experienced both the benefits and the tensions of immigration, which is what the novel's setting shows.

In conclusion, Syal shows through Tollington a society in transition. The mining village, the motorway and the attack on an Indian man show a community in which anxiety about change is turned against people seen as different. Yet the village is also where Meena learns to value her heritage, so the setting is both a source of the novel's tension and the place where her identity grows.
