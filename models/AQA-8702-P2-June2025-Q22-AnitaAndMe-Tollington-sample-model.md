## Provisional study response

Syal opens Anita and Me by showing a village that has already been changed once and is about to change again. Tollington is a place defined by what it has lost, and its people are anxious about what is arriving. Through Meena, a nine-year-old who is the daughter of the only Indian family, Syal shows a society in which economic decline, new arrivals and old habits meet, and in which fear of change often turns into prejudice. I will argue that the setting works as a small version of changing Britain, and that it is also where Meena first learns how it feels to be an outsider who wants to belong.

The first chapter builds the village's past through the mine. Syal writes that "The Tollington mine had once employed the whole village", and she makes the link between place and people intimate: "The mine and the village had been as intertwined as lovers". The simile treats the industry as a relationship, so its end is felt as a loss rather than a change in employment figures. The narrator states it flatly: "Then the mine suddenly closed down in the late Fifties". The short sentence and the word "suddenly" suggest a change that nobody in the community chose or understood. Most of the working men leave, so that the village becomes "a community of tough, broad-armed women and fragile old men". The adjectives are paired to show a community that has lost its working middle and survives on strength and fragility.

Into this gap come new people. The same sentence continues that "a few new families started moving in, drawn by the country air and dirt-cheap housing", and Meena adds "families like us". The Kumars are therefore part of the change, one of the families who arrive because the village is cheap. Syal does not make the arrival dramatic, but the reader sees that the old Tollington is being replaced by a more mixed one without any plan. Meena's own sense of difference is clear in small details, for example that her front garden of her mother's herbs was "the odd one out in the village" among the crowded gardens of her neighbours. A garden is the most ordinary form of belonging, and Meena's embarrassment about it shows that she already measures herself against the village's norms.

Change in the village is also economic, and it affects who has power. The factory "had opened, by way of compensation, soon after the mine closure", yet it was "not the men they wanted; they wanted women". The women who now work there are known as "The Ballbearings Committee", a name that is humorous but also shows that the village has made a group out of them. Their husbands are described as "incidental", so the factory gives the women work and a shared identity that the men no longer have. Syal presents a society adjusting to a new order, with energy that has "nowhere to put it". The ballbearing image, bouncing against the sides of a pinball machine, suggests a community that has force but is boxed in by its boundaries.

The setting is also closed and watchful. When papa threatens to ask Mr Ormerod what happened, a door slams somewhere along the terrace and Meena says that "Everyone must have been watching, they always did, what else was there to do?" The rhetorical question makes surveillance a way of life. In a village like this, a girl who is different is always seen, and prejudice can spread without needing anyone to say a word. The same chapter shows how it is spoken: Meena is punished at school after she reacts to a racist remark from a classmate during a lesson on why the area is called the Black Country. Syal places the prejudice in a classroom and in a history lesson, as if the community's account of its own past already contains the idea that some people do not belong.

The Big House adds another side of the village. It is "the only building set apart from the main road", behind a fence, and Meena concludes that whoever lived there "would not break their solitude to save a little Indian girl". Its owner never cared to mix with the neighbours, so the one house with power over the village shares nothing of its grief. Meena's isolation within the village is matched by the isolation of its most powerful house.

Later in the novel the same pressures become more serious. According to Bitesize and the OCR guide, plans for a motorway that will destroy parts of Tollington make people angry, and Sam Lowbridge, who is usually kind to Meena, shouts racist insults at the local fete and later says something racist into a television camera, after which an Indian man overseeing the work is attacked and robbed. The anger about a physical change, the loss of part of the village, is turned against a person. A road is a change that ordinary villagers cannot control, and the fear it causes is directed at someone from outside the community. Meena's friendship with Anita is also damaged, because Anita is impressed by Sam's racism, and Meena is shocked when she overhears her describe a racist attack. Meena's grandmother Nanima arrives from India, tells her stories and teaches her Punjabi, and helps her to feel pride in her heritage. By the end Meena has told the truth about the pond, passed the eleven-plus, and her family moves to Wolverhampton. She leaves changed, with a firmer sense of her heritage.

Context supports this reading. Syal was born in 1963 and grew up in a mining village in the West Midlands, and the novel is semi-autobiographical. After the Second World War Britain encouraged immigration from Europe and the Commonwealth, and the Commonwealth Immigrants Act of 1962 then limited it. The OCR guide describes the decline of the industries of the Black Country as part of the background. Read alongside this context, Tollington's lost mine and its new families can be seen as a small version of a wider national change, though that is one reading drawn from the guides and the opening chapter.

In conclusion, the opening chapter shows Tollington as a society in transition: the mine's closure, the factory that employs women, and the arrival of families like the Kumars show a community that has changed without being asked. Its watchfulness makes the village difficult for Meena, and, according to the guides, later episodes show fear of change turning against people seen as different. On that evidence Tollington is both a source of the novel's tension and the place where Meena begins to understand herself.
