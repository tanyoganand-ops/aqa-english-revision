# Question 10 (30 marks): Jane Eyre

**Question:** see the official paper for the full wording (linked above).

## Model answer

Brontë presents Jane as very vulnerable as a child, and as vulnerable in a different way as an adult. But she also shows a girl who judges her treatment as unjust and who learns to refuse it. Jane's weakness is mostly a matter of her situation, and her strength is a matter of her mind.

In the extract, Jane is physically trapped and made small. Bessie and Miss Abbot "left me riveted" on the ottoman, and "riveted" is a word for metal fixed in place. When she tests the door she finds "no jail was ever more secure", so a nursery punishment becomes a prison. The room is large and cold: it is "one of the largest and stateliest chambers in the mansion", with a bed "like a tabernacle" and a chair "like a pale throne". The words are those of church and kingship, and they make the room seem far bigger than a child. Brontë uses colour for the same effect. The bed hangs are "deep red damask", and out of these "rose high, and glared white, the piled-up mattresses and pillows". The red is heavy and the white is cold, and Jane sits between the two.

The mirror is the most important detail. Jane sees "the strange little figure there gazing at me, with a white face and arms specking the gloom, and glittering eyes of fear". Her own image seems like "a real spirit", so she cannot even trust her own reflection. The adjective "little" and the verb "specking" make her tiny against the "vacant majesty" of the room. The red-room was also where Mr Reed died, and "a sense of dreary consecration had guarded it", so she is placed in a room tied to a death.

The rest of Chapter 2 gives the reason for her weakness. Jane is told "you are less than a servant, for you do nothing for your keep". She is a dependant in a house that does not want her, and she knows it. She asks "Why was I always suffering, always browbeaten, always accused, forever condemned?" The four-part list builds into a cry, and the question has no answer. Later she says "I was a discord in Gateshead Hall: I was like nobody there", which shows that her isolation is also a matter of belonging. Yet Brontë gives her a reply too: "Unjust!—unjust!" said my reason. A child who can name injustice has a kind of strength.

Jane's adult vulnerability is mostly about money and status. A British Library article on the figure of the governess (Kathryn Hughes) notes that the 1851 Census recorded 25,000 women earning their living as governesses, that most lived with their employers on a small salary, and that she was neither quite family nor quite servant. Jane's route to Thornfield follows that path, and at Thornfield she is both an employee and a person Rochester wants to marry. When she leaves him she has nothing: she says "I am absolutely destitute". The word "absolutely" shows that this is not just a feeling. It is a fact, and Brontë places her at the crossroads of Whitcross, a place with no town, to make the point.

Her strength shows at the moments she is most at risk. She tells Rochester "I am no bird; and no net ensnares me; I am a free human being with an independent will". The negatives and the bird image reject the idea that she can be caught, and the line makes her will the thing that is not vulnerable. At Thornfield she also asserts that "women feel just as men feel", which is an argument against the limits placed on her sex.

The ending complicates the question. When she returns to Rochester she says "I am independent, sir, as well as rich: I am my own mistress." Her independence is real, but it comes with a legacy, so Brontë links her freedom to her money. That suggests she was vulnerable because she was poor, and that she is secure now partly because she is not. It is one reading, but the novel does not let her moral strength alone protect her.

In conclusion, Brontë presents Jane as vulnerable to a large extent: she is an orphan, a dependant, a woman with few ways to earn a living and a child shut in a room. Yet the same novel gives her a clear sense of justice and a will that does not bend, so I would say she is vulnerable in her circumstances and strong in herself.

## Notes (my reading)

- AO1: one argument (circumstance makes Jane vulnerable, her mind does not), tied to the extract and to Gateshead, Thornfield, Whitcross and the ending.
- AO2: "riveted", the prison image, the church and throne words, the colour contrast, the mirror, the list of four questions and the bird image are analysed as methods.
- AO3: the context is the position of the governess, from the British Library article "The figure of the governess" by Kathryn Hughes (1851 Census figure, small salary, living with the employer, an unclear place between family and servant). It is used to explain why Jane's route and her money matter, as one reading. The claim is kept narrow and is not a claim about every governess or about Brontë's intention. No single context or topic is required.
- Section B has no AO4 mark.
- Limits: the answer is about 760 words, longer than a typical timed answer. Quotations beyond the extract were checked against the Standard Ebooks text only. The British Library page was read as fetched on 26 Nov 2026. "Women feel just as men feel" is from the novel's text (Chapter 12), not the extract. The answer does not discuss Lowood or St John Rivers in detail, which is a choice of focus.
