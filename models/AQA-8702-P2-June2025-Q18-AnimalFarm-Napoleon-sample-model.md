## Model answer

Orwell presents Napoleon as the main cause of the revolution's failure, because he takes power, uses fear and lies to keep it, and ends by becoming what the animals first overthrew. But Orwell does not blame him alone. Other animals make his rise easier, and I think this makes the novel a warning about more than one bad leader.

Napoleon's character is set up early. He is "not much of a talker, but with a reputation for getting his own way". The first half of the description makes him quiet and the second half makes him controlling, so Orwell tells us at the start that his power does not rest on argument. Later he acts in secret. After Jessie and Bluebell give birth, "Napoleon took them away from their mothers", and he keeps the puppies in a loft until the farm forgets them. The verb "took" is blunt and shows that he does not ask. When the dogs return, "nine enormous dogs" chase Snowball from the barn, and the number and size of the dogs give Napoleon force. Orwell adds that the dogs "wagged their tails to him in the same way as the other dogs had been used to do to Mr. Jones". The comparison tells the reader that the new master has the same kind of loyalty from his own animals as the old one did.

Napoleon is also the animal who is most responsible for ending free debate. Once Snowball is gone, he stops the Sunday discussions, and when the windmill is wrecked he blames Snowball: "this traitor has crept here under cover of night". The word "traitor" gives him an enemy to blame, and the animals' fear is then turned away from him. Later, after the confessions, "a pile of corpses" lies "before Napoleon's feet". The image places the blame for the killings with him, and it contradicts Old Major's warning that "no animal must ever tyrannise over his own kind". The animals only "thought they remembered" the Sixth Commandment, "No animal shall kill any other animal", and their doubt keeps them quiet.

Squealer is how Napoleon controls what the animals know. Orwell says that Squealer "could turn black into white", and the idiom shows that he can make a lie sound true. When the other animals are unhappy about the pigs' use of the farmhouse bed, the animals find that the Fourth Commandment reads "No animal shall sleep in a bed with sheets", and Squealer explains why. The added words are small, which is the point: each change is too small to protest. Napoleon does not need to speak. Orwell notes that Napoleon "was not seen in public as often as once in a fortnight", and orders come "through Squealer". The distance makes him seem powerful and untouchable. At the end, the sheep chant "Four legs good, two legs BETTER" just as the pigs appear on two legs, and the capital letters and the repetition show how the noise drowns the animals' protest. The final Commandment, "ALL ANIMALS ARE EQUAL BUT SOME ANIMALS ARE MORE EQUAL THAN OTHERS", shows the failure most clearly, because the word "but" cancels the first half. In the last lines the animals outside look "from pig to man", and it is "impossible to say which was which", so the revolution has become the thing it replaced. The farm's name is even changed back: "the name 'Animal Farm' had been abolished".

However, the revolution is not destroyed by Napoleon alone. Boxer's maxim "I will work harder" is a good example. It is a loyal and generous motto, but it is also a way of not asking questions, and when Boxer says "If Comrade Napoleon says it, it must be right", his trust helps Napoleon more than any order. Napoleon betrays him in the end. The van is marked "Alfred Simmonds, Horse Slaughterer and Glue Boiler", and the animals are too late to save Boxer. The cruelty is Napoleon's, but the earlier silence was shared. The pigs also act together. When the pigs take the milk and apples, "even Snowball and Napoleon" agree, so the pigs' privilege does not begin with Napoleon alone. This suggests that the failure is a failure of the whole group of pigs and of the animals who do not resist.

The context supports this reading. The Orwell Foundation describes the novel, written in 1943 to 1944 and published in 1945, as an allegory on the Soviet regime. The historian Robert Pearce, writing in History Today in 2005, identifies Napoleon with Stalin and Snowball with Trotsky, though he also warns that the parallels are not exact. Pearce says that many readers see the animals other than the pigs as innocent dupes, and that Orwell shows Boxer as someone who does not have the intelligence to stand up to Napoleon. I use this as one reading, not as proof of what Orwell meant to do in each scene. It suggests that Napoleon stands for the way a revolution can be taken over by one leader, while the other animals show how a population can be kept quiet.

In conclusion, Orwell presents Napoleon as largely responsible for the failure. He takes the dogs, silences Snowball, controls speech through Squealer and has animals killed after the confessions. But Orwell also shows the animals' tiredness, trust and poor memory, and the pigs' shared greed, so I would say that Napoleon is the chief cause but not the only one.

## Notes (my reading, not an examiner result)

- AO1: one argument (Napoleon is the chief cause, but the pigs and the trusting animals share the blame), supported by the dogs, Snowball's expulsion, the executions, the Commandments and Boxer.
- AO2: characterisation, verbs, the idiom for Squealer, the capitals in the sheep's chant, the final line and the changing Commandments are analysed as methods.
- AO3: the context is the Soviet allegory, from the Orwell Foundation page on Animal Farm (https://www.orwellfoundation.com/the-orwell-foundation/orwell/books-by-orwell/animal-farm/) and Robert Pearce, "Animal Farm and Soviet Satire", History Today, August 2005 (https://www.historytoday.com/archive/feature/animal-farm-and-soviet-satire). Both pages were read as fetched on 26 Nov 2026. The identification of Napoleon with Stalin is Pearce's, and he notes that the parallels are not exact. No single context or topic is required.
