# Question 13 (30 marks): The Sign of Four

**Question:** see the official paper for the full wording (linked above).

## Model answer

Conan Doyle creates mystery by giving the reader half a story, and suspense by cutting it off at the moment it would become clear. The extract does this in a few lines, and the rest of the novel repeats it.

In the extract, Thaddeus is about to give the answer to the whole puzzle. His father says "Put your ears down to my mouth. The treasure is hidden in –" and the dash stops the sentence. The reader leans in with the son and is refused. The next sentence is a sudden change: "a horrible change came over his expression; his eyes stared wildly, his jaw dropped". The verbs are short and physical, and then comes the cry, "Keep him out! For Christ's sake keep him out!" The repeated command, the exclamation marks and the oath show panic, and the cry sounds all the more terrifying because we do not know who "him" is.

The face at the window is described slowly, so that fear can build. "A face was looking in at us out of the darkness." The detail "the whitening of the nose where it was pressed against the glass" is small and exact, which makes it believable. The adjectives "wild", "cruel" and "bearded, hairy" make a figure out of an animal and a villain, and "an expression of concentrated malevolence" gives the reader the feeling without a name. Then it is gone: "the man was gone". The old man is dead by the time they turn back, so the one person who could explain has died, and the reader has a new question for every answer.

Conan Doyle gives only fragments of proof. There is "a single foot-mark" in the flower-bed, and the narrator says "But for that one trace, we might have thought that our imaginations had conjured up that wild, fierce face." This is a clue and a doubt at once. Then the paper pinned to the chest: "The sign of the four". Thaddeus says there were "secret agencies at work all round us", and at the end of his account he says it is "still a complete mystery". The phrase "secret agencies" suggests an organised, hidden enemy, and nothing the brothers say explains it.

The way the account is told adds to this. It is a story inside a story, told by Thaddeus while he relights his hookah, and the narrator Watson is only a listener. "We had all sat absorbed, listening to his extraordinary narrative." Because Watson knows no more than the reader, we share his lack of information, and Mary Morstan's reaction, "turned deadly white", shows that the story is tied to her own lost father.

The wider novel uses the same methods. The mystery begins with a letter to Mary that says "You are a wronged woman, and shall have justice. Do not bring police." Holmes calls it "a very pretty little mystery", so the reader is invited to enjoy it, while the warning makes it sinister. At Pondicherry Lodge the clue is a face again: Watson sees "a face—the very face of our companion Thaddeus", with "a horrible smile, a fixed and unnatural grin, which in that still and moonlit room was more jarring to the nerves than any scowl or contortion". The dead man's smile is more frightening than anger, because it cannot be explained. Holmes says "There is something devilish in this, Watson", and he is "more moved than I had ever before seen him", so even the detective is shaken.

Holmes is also why the mystery matters. At the start, he calls the world "dreary, dismal, unprofitable" and complains that there is no "field upon which to exert" his powers. A great detective needs a great puzzle, so the mystery is also a way of showing what Holmes can do. The suspense then comes from waiting for him to reduce it to reasoning, and the answers are delivered in the voice of a man who has already worked them out.

Context supports a further reading. The UNESCO World Heritage tentative-list entry for the Andaman penal settlement says that the settlement was set up after the 1857-58 uprising and that its first convicts landed at Port Blair in March 1858. In the novel, Jonathan Small's time as a convict on the Andaman Islands is part of the hidden past behind the crime. So the "face" and the "sign" come from a past that English characters have tried to bury. I read the mystery as partly about what the empire had left behind, though this is a reading and not a claim about Conan Doyle's intentions.

In conclusion, Conan Doyle creates mystery with broken sentences, partial clues and faces seen at night, and suspense by delaying each answer until the reader needs it. The pattern runs from the extract to the end of the novel, and it shows Holmes as the one person who can read the signs.

## Notes (my reading)

- AO1: one argument (mystery comes from partial information and suspense from delay), tied to the extract, the letter to Mary, Pondicherry Lodge and Holmes's opening complaint.
- AO2: the broken sentence, the verbs, the repeated cry, the slowly built description of the face, the footprint and the paper, the story-within-a-story frame and Watson's position are analysed as methods.
- AO3: the context is the UNESCO World Heritage tentative-list entry "Colonial Penal Settlement of Andaman Islands" (settlement established after the 1857-58 uprising, first convicts landed March 1858). It is used only for the historical setting of the Andaman past in the plot, as one reading. I do not claim anything about Conan Doyle's intention. No single context or topic is required.
