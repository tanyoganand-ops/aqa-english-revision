## Model answer

Kelly uses Leah as the one member of the group who keeps asking the moral question, and then shows how little her asking achieves. The other teenagers respond to Adam's apparent death with plans, threats and blame. Leah talks to a boyfriend who will not answer, challenges him, and finally leaves. Through her, Kelly explores a disturbing idea about human nature: that people can see clearly that something is wrong and still be carried along by someone who offers them safety.

The relationship with Phil is where Kelly sets up the idea. Leah's scenes with Phil are built as a series of monologues, because she speaks to him and he gives no verbal reply. Phil, who spends much of the play eating and drinking, seems to listen without responding, and Leah is insecure in the relationship: much of her speech is an attempt to get any reaction from him. The form makes the point before the content does. Leah is the character with the most to say about right and wrong, and she is placed beside the one character who has the power to change what happens and refuses to speak. The imbalance suggests that conscience is weak when nobody with authority will listen to it.

Leah's speech about bonobos, which closes the first section, shows how Kelly uses her to widen the play beyond one crime. The speech is not about the immediate plot. It sets human behaviour beside animal behaviour, and because it follows the account of the bullying and the plan to cover up Adam's death, it invites the audience to ask whether the gang behaves better or worse than animals. Kelly lets Leah end the section by saying repeatedly that they are all in trouble now. The repetition is a small piece of structure with a large effect: it makes the end of the first section feel like a verdict. I read this as Kelly giving the play a moral frame through its least powerful character, although it is one interpretation and the speech can be read in other ways.

In the second section Leah's questions become more personal. She asks Phil 'What have we done?' and the group's guilt is put into plain words, yet Phil does not answer. Later she tells him that if you change just one thing, you can change the world, a hopeful idea that meets the same silence. Kelly places these moments close to scenes in which the group does something far worse: an innocent man is framed through false DNA evidence, and Phil threatens Brian until he agrees to help. The contrast is deliberate. Leah offers the group a way to think about choice and responsibility, while the group chooses fear and control, and the audience sees which one wins.

The play's most shocking turn gives Leah her hardest test. When Adam is found alive, he is shown speaking haltingly and barely able to remember his name. Phil, rather than repair what has been done, takes charge of a plan to kill him and demonstrates it with a plastic bag, using the oblivious Brian. He justifies himself with an appeal to the group: 'I'm in charge. Everyone is happier.' The claim sounds reassuring, which is the danger in it. Phil presents the plan as the way to keep everyone safe, and the group follows. Leah tries to reason with him and he leaves without answering. At the point where the play's ethical dilemma becomes most extreme, the person who objects has no means of stopping it. Kelly seems to ask how far self-protection can be allowed to go, and Leah's failure suggests that the group's answer is: as far as it takes.

Leah's loyalty to Adam sharpens the contrast. She reminds Phil how long they have known him, and at one point tells Phil 'you're not human'. The line is an accusation, and it also raises the play's question about human nature. If Phil's behaviour is inhuman, then Kelly is showing that people are capable of it, since he is a teenager among other teenagers. Leah's words give the audience the language to judge, but the other characters do not take it up.

Her departure completes this pattern, and the order of events matters. At the end of the third section Leah is alone with Phil, begins to cry and storms off when he tries to comfort her. As she goes, Phil addresses her directly, which is the only time he does so, and it comes too late. In the fourth section it is Jan and Mark, opening as they open every section, who tell the audience that Leah has presumably gone, having moved schools. The scene in the field is then between Phil and Richard, a kind of substitute for Leah, who asks Phil to 'Come back, Phil'. That line is Richard's, not Leah's, and Phil still does not respond. Leah's exit is the only way she can refuse to take part, but it does not change what happened to Adam, and the play ends with Phil and Richard in silence. Kelly suggests that withdrawing may protect the person who leaves without protecting anyone else. That is my inference, not a stated fact.

Placing Leah beside other characters adds to the argument. John Tate is a bully who panics and loses control when he is faced with Adam's death, and Danny's fear is about his future as a dentist. Leah is different because her concern is for Adam. Even so, Kelly does not let her stand as a hero: she stays with the group for most of the play, and the play shows that the group's loyalty to Phil is stronger than her disapproval. It is therefore a hard view of human nature, in which the voice of conscience exists but loses to leadership based on fear.

Context gives the argument some weight, though it should not replace the analysis. Pearson's guide, an exam-board teaching resource, reads the play as one in which communication between characters keeps failing, so that exchanges become a series of monologues, and it says Leah is one of the characters who might have offered a counterargument to Phil's plotting but who moves schools instead. The same guide notes that the two boys who killed James Bulger in 1993 were ten years old, younger than any of Kelly's characters, and uses that to suggest the play asks audiences to recognise how much of young people's experience adults never see. I treat this as one commentator's reading, not as Kelly's stated intention. It supports a reading in which Leah's failure to be heard is part of the play's point about how groups and the people around them can ignore warning voices.

In conclusion, Kelly uses Leah to carry the play's moral questions, through her one-sided conversations with Phil, her speech about bonobos, her questions about what the group has done and her failure to stop the plan. Her departure shows that conscience can survive in one person while failing to change the group, which is a bleak idea about how people behave under pressure.

## Notes (my reading, not an examiner result)

- AO1: the evidence used comes from Leah's one-sided scenes with Phil, her long speech on animals, her questions in the second section, her reaction to the plan for Adam, and her exit. Events follow the Pearson guide's summary.
- AO2: Leah's speech against Phil's silence, the monologue form of their scenes, speeches that step outside the action, the repetition that closes the first section, and the contrast with the group's actions.
- AO3: ideas (human and animal behaviour, extreme behaviour under threat, limits of self-protection, bullying and leadership) are argued from events. Context comes only from the Pearson guide (communication failure and monologue form; the 1993 Bulger case as a real-world comparison the guide draws). This is secondary-source context, hedged as one commentator's reading. I do not claim AO3 is met by ideas alone.
- AO4: aimed at accurate spelling, punctuation and varied vocabulary. Not scored.
- Source limit: the quoted phrases come only from the Pearson guide's own quotations of the play and could not be checked against the playscript.
- Timeline caution: the guide places Phil's one direct address to Leah "when she leaves" and refers to "the end of the play" in one place; I tie it to her exit at the end of the third section and word it briefly. Word count about 1,150, longer than a timed answer.
