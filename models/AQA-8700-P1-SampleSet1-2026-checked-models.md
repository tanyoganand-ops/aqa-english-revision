# Question 2 (8 marks): language

**Question:** see the official paper for the full wording (linked above).

### Model answer

The writer describes the hyena as a creature that looks wrong in every detail, and the narrator's tone makes the description both comic and uneasy.

The paragraph opens with a pretended fairness. The narrator says he holds no prejudice against any animal (line 10), which sounds reasonable, but the plain statement of fact that follows (line 10) lets him present his judgement as if no one could disagree. The blunt verdict that the animal is ugly beyond saving (line 11) follows as a short sentence. "Ugly" is a plain, harsh adjective, and "redemption" is a word normally used about saving a person from sin. By using it of an animal's looks, the writer makes the hyena seem almost damned, and prepares us to fear it.

The writer then builds the picture by listing parts, and each part is something spoiled. The coat is a "bungled" mix of colours (line 12). "Bungled" is a verb for a clumsy job, so the hyena seems badly made, which could provoke disgust or a little pity. The spots are set against a leopard's "ostentation" (line 13), a contrast that sets the hyena against an animal we think of as beautiful, and "ostentation" gives the leopard's beauty a showy air, so the hyena is condemned for lacking what is hardly admirable. The spots are also compared to the symptoms of an illness (lines 13 to 14), a simile that links the animal with disease.

Pi's wit shows in the humour. The head is likened to a bear's, with a receding hairline (line 15). The verb "suffering" is deliberately grand for baldness, so the comparison mocks while keeping a touch of sympathy. The ears are described with an exaggerating adverb (lines 15 to 16), and a reference to loss in battle (line 16) brings in violence as a casual aside.

The sentence forms change the pace. After longer, detailed sentences, short flat ones follow about the nostrils (line 17) and the tail (lines 17 to 18). These read like a list of faults being ticked off. The negative "unwagging" lacks the friendliness of a dog's tail, and the paragraph closes by calling the hyena doglike but unlike any dog anyone would want (line 18). Everything about the hyena is almost familiar and wrong, and that gives the description its mixture of comedy and threat.

### Notes (my reading, not an examiner result)

- Aim: effects of choices analysed, a range of detail, terminology used accurately (adjective, verb, simile, adverb, antithesis, declarative sentences). It stays inside lines 10 to 19 and on the hyena's appearance, as the task requires.
- It does not cover every possible point; the content is a guide, not a checklist.

# Question 3 (8 marks): structure to create tension

**Question:** see the official paper for the full wording (linked above).

### Model answer

The writer builds tension by planting a fear in the first sentence, delaying the threat with description, making it appear suddenly, letting it escalate, and then releasing the tension in a way that changes how we read it.

The extract begins with Pi saying that the hyena is what worried him (line 1). The statement is placed first, so the reader knows what to fear before anything happens. Pi's memory of his father's words (line 1) adds exposition: hyenas attack in packs (line 2) and are formidable attackers (line 4), so the reader can judge the danger for themselves. The first person narration puts us inside Pi's fear, and we only see what he sees.

Next the focus moves from threat to description. The whole of the second paragraph is about the hyena's looks (see line 11 onwards). Nothing happens, and the delay works because we already know what the hyena could do. The comic tone of the description sits uneasily with the fear of the first paragraph, which is a shift in tone that keeps us unsure how to feel.

The pivotal point comes when the hyena suddenly leaps over the zebra (lines 20 to 21). The adverb of speed and the sudden verb make the change from waiting to action, and Pi's earlier remark that he was disappointed (line 20) shows that his hope in the first paragraph failed. After this, the writer makes the action repeat and grow: the hyena runs laps (line 26), and the counting stretches the sentence out, and the repeated sound (line 27) is a motif that returns later (line 40), so the threat becomes something Pi cannot escape. Small details sharpen it: Pi tenses (line 31) and his heart jumps (line 41), showing Pi's body reacting at each lap, so tension builds by repetition rather than by a new event.

The end changes the pattern. The hyena makes groans, vomits (lines 43 to 45), and stays in the restricted space (line 50). The threat falls away in an anti-climax. The tension is released, but it leaves an odd feeling, because the fear Pi has shown for so long now looks out of proportion to the animal's "anguish" (line 49). The writer ends not on danger but on the hyena's pitiful state, so the reader is left with a changed view of the creature, and with the question of what will come next on the boat.

### Notes (my reading, not an examiner result)

- Covers these structural features: foreshadowing, exposition, shift of focus, pivotal point, motif, rising action, anti-climax, first-person perspective. It ties each to the effect on the reader.
- Written for the 2026 sample wording (a tension focus). A response to the June 2025 interest focus would need a different focus word, though the same structural features would be relevant.

# Question 4 (20 marks): evaluation

**Question:** see the official paper for the full wording (linked above).

### Model answer

I partly agree. The hyena behaves in ways that are comic, and the ending shows it is no danger to Pi. But the writer lets us feel Pi's fear for most of the extract, and I do not think the hyena is simply a joke. The comedy arrives late and changes the meaning of what came before.

The comic side is strong. The hyena runs round the zebra as if the boat were a running track (line 25). The image of the track belongs to sport, not hunting, so the predator is made to look like a runner in training. The counting of laps (lines 25 to 26) mimics a commentator, and the repeated sound (line 27) is a silly, high-pitched noise. The repetition is not frightening: the more it is repeated, the more trivial the sound becomes. The animal is also confused, whimpering and hesitating before it begins (line 22), which is not how a pack hunter behaves.

Yet the writer keeps the threat in play. Pi reports his fear in plain, short sentences: he prepares for his end (lines 37 to 38) and his heart jumps (line 41). The hyena is described with strong verbs and sounds, the beating of its legs and its clicking claws (lines 30 to 31), so it is physically powerful, and Pi ends by fixing on the animal as a "maniacal" beast (line 43). Because the story is told in the first person, we see everything through Pi's terror, so even absurd behaviour feels dangerous to us. The glance at Pi (lines 35 to 36) is the most frightening moment, as the hyena's look is calm and fixed. And the circling could suggest a predator preparing to attack, which Pi has been told hyenas do.

The ending decides the question. The hyena groans, coughs and vomits (lines 43 to 47), then drops into what it has just produced (line 48). The sudden anti-climax gives a verdict on the threat: it is not a hunter but a distressed animal at the limit of its distress (line 49). That phrase is grand and almost sorrowful, so the writer makes us shift from laughing to something like pity. Pi still pushes himself away from it (line 45), so his fear remains, and we realise how much of the tension came from his expectations of what the hyena could do, rather than from what it did.

So the statement is right that the hyena does no harm in these lines and that its behaviour can be funny. It is incomplete in calling the hyena a threat of no importance at the time, because the writer places the reader in Pi's position, where the threat is felt until the very end. The extract works because the comedy and the fear are in tension with each other.

### Notes (my reading, not an examiner result)

- Aim: it takes a clear position, uses both sides of the AO4 content, evaluates the effect on the reader, and ties points to quotations and methods (first-person perspective, repetition and onomatopoeia, verbs, anti-climax).
- It stays within lines 20 to the end.
- About 560 words. Teaching model, not a timed target.

# Question 5 (40 marks): descriptive writing, option 1

**Question:** see the official paper for the full wording (linked above).

### Model piece (option 1, a description, about 350 words)

Nobody tells you that a wildlife park wakes before you do. At six, the gates are still chained, but the air behind them is already moving: a low cough from the far paddock, a rattle of hooves, the slow percussion of something large shifting its weight.

By the time the first visitors arrive, the park has settled into its daytime face. Gravel paths curve between enclosures as if the whole place were drawn with a soft pencil. The elephants' yard is a bowl of pale dust, trodden into small craters, where a calf drags a branch twice its own length from one side to the other and back again, quite certain that this matters. Beside it the water in the lake lies flat and green. The flamingos stand in it on one leg each, a hundred mirrored question marks.

Everything here is somewhere else. The roofs of the monkey house are shaped like a thatched village that no monkey has ever seen. The signs on the fences tell you where each animal belongs, in Kenya, in Sumatra, in the Andes, and they sound slightly apologetic. The smell changes every few steps: warm straw, then sour musk, then the sweetness of cut grass, then something wet and wild from the otters' pool.

Children press their palms against the glass and the lions look straight through them. A male lies on a shelf of sun-warmed rock, his heavy head on his paws, and every so often an ear flicks, as if the day were a fly. His stillness is not tame. It is the stillness of something that has decided, for now, to wait.

By late afternoon the light slants gold through the netting of the aviary. The parrots clatter and quarrel. A keeper moves quietly along a fence, checking a latch, and the animals watch her more closely than they watch us.

When the gates close, the park lets out a long breath. The paths empty, the lamps come on in their small yellow circles, and from somewhere past the dark paddocks comes one slow, deep sound, rolling over the roofs and the fences and the sleeping town beyond, to remind everyone whose place this is.

### Notes (my reading, not an examiner result)

- AO5: a clear structure (dawn, day, closing) with a return to the opening image, sensory detail, a controlled tone, and ideas about captivity and place. AO6: varied sentence lengths, accurate punctuation, and ambitious vocabulary that is meant to be used with control. I make no claim about marks.
- Original text, written for this model. The picture option is not used.
